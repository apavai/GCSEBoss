When Are Year 11 Mocks? What the Timetable Means for Your Autumn

When are Year 11 mocks? There is no national date to look up. Your school's letter is the timetable that matters.

Mid September is when many Year 11 households get the first mock calendar. It can land as a PDF, a planner page, or a line in an email. The useful job this week is not to invent a revision marathon around when are Year 11 mocks. It is to read the timetable calmly and decide what autumn needs to protect.

When are Year 11 mocks, really?

Mocks are set by the school. Not by an exam board, and not by a government calendar you can Google once and trust.

Some schools sit a first mock block in November. Others wait until December or early January. A few run short subject mocks earlier. Sister schools in the same trust can still pick different weeks.

So if a neighbour's child sits English next month and yours does not, that is normal. Comparing dates across WhatsApp groups usually creates noise, not a plan.

What the mock timetable is actually for

The letter is a planning tool. Three questions usually pay for themselves.

1. Does it clash with a holiday you already booked?

Scan half term, family trips, and work away weeks against the mock window. If a clash is real, email the school early with the date and the subject list. Do not wait until the week before.

Most schools can advise on what is possible. They cannot invent a second sitting if nobody asks.

2. Which subjects sit first?

Order matters for energy at home. A heavy science week followed by English is a different household than three lighter subjects with gaps.

You do not need a colour coded wall chart. You do need to know which evenings will feel full, and which weekends are still free.

3. When is the last free weekend before the block starts?

That weekend is useful for sleep, a short catch up on one stuck topic, or nothing at all. Protecting it beats starting a six week panic plan tonight.

Mocks are information, not a verdict

A mock tells the department where gaps sit under timed conditions. It is not a final grade. It is not a character assessment. It is not a prophecy about summer 2027.

Some students score lower than expected because the course is incomplete. Some score higher because the paper favoured topics they have just finished. Either way, the next step is a short conversation with the teacher about what to practise next, not a house-wide meltdown.

If your child is anxious already, keep the language small. "We will read the letter together." "We will ask one question if something looks wrong." Skip the speeches about "this is when it gets real."

What not to do when the letter arrives

Do not invent a national date and then correct it later. There isn't one for Year 11 mocks.

Do not rewrite their timetable into a May countdown. Summer exams for this cohort are still a long way off, and countdown culture usually raises stress without adding skill.

Do not turn parents evening into a mock tribunal. Use the slot for concrete next steps, not for arguing with a paper that has not been sat yet.

Do not buy a new system every time a date appears. One exercise book per subject and a clear place for the letter is enough for this week.

A calm autumn plan that fits mid September

  1. Put the mock dates in the family calendar the same day the letter arrives.
  2. Highlight the first subject and the last free weekend.
  3. Ask your child which subject they feel least ready for, then stop after one short answer.
  4. If a holiday clash exists, email the school once, with dates.
  5. Leave technique deep dives for later. This post is the calendar piece, not a mock revision method guide.

That is the whole habit. It takes ten minutes. It prevents three weeks of vague worry.

How schools usually communicate dates

Look in the places your school already uses: parent app, email, planner, website calendar. If nothing has landed by late September and your child says friends have dates, ask form tutor or the year office once. A polite "Have mock dates gone out for Year 11 yet?" is enough.

Do not assume silence means "no mocks this year." It more often means the block is still being finalised.

Brothers, sisters, and shared calendars

If you have more than one child in exam years, stack the calendars before you promise trips. A Year 11 mock week and a sibling's primary play can collide hard.

Share the letter with whoever does school runs. A date only on one phone helps nobody on a Tuesday morning.

What teachers hope parents do with the timetable

Quiet support. Sleep protected. One stuck topic named. No surprise weekend tutoring booked without checking what the department already set.

They also hope you will not treat the mock as a second GCSE. The paper is a rehearsal for timing and for seeing what still needs teaching. That is a school job first.

If the dates change

Timetables move. Rooms change. A subject gets moved earlier. When an update arrives, replace the old calendar entry the same day. Do not keep two versions alive in the group chat.

If a change creates a new holiday clash, write again. One clear email beats three anxious messages.

A short script for the kitchen table

"We've got the mock dates. Let's put them on the calendar, check holidays, and note which subject is first. Then we leave it for tonight."

That script is boring on purpose. Boring is what mid September needs.

The timetable itself is the job

When are Year 11 mocks? Whenever your school says they are. Read the letter for clashes, subject order, and the last free weekend. Treat the block as information for the autumn, not as a verdict on your child.
