Starting Year 11: The Set-Up Week That Saves You in May

Nobody's asking you to revise this week. Genuinely. But there's a short list of boring admin that takes about two hours now and quietly removes most of the friction from the year ahead. Here's the whole thing.

Starting Year 11 isn't harder than Year 10 in the way people like to warn you about. The work steps up a bit, sure. What really changes is the amount of admin bolted onto it: mock dates, coursework deadlines, spec lists, and a lot of paper that has to live somewhere. Ask anyone who's already been through it and the stress they describe is usually twenty minutes hunting for a worksheet, or getting to March and realising nobody ever checked which exam board they were on.

So, a set-up list rather than a revision plan. Two hours in the last week of the holiday, spread across whatever days you fancy, and then you're done thinking about it. And no, this isn't a lecture about how you spent August. August is for August.

Why the week before starting Year 11 is worth two hours

Here's the rough maths. Say you lose ten minutes a week to hunting for something, or to not quite knowing what you're meant to be doing. Across a Year 11 teaching year of roughly 33 weeks, that's about five and a half hours, and that's the optimistic version.

Then there are the bigger one-off costs. The week in January you spend rebuilding a system because the first one fell over. The fortnight in April where half your revision turns out to be aimed at the wrong topics. Those hurt considerably more than the ten minutes do.

None of that comes down to how hard you work. Setup problems are the cheap kind, because you only ever fix them once. Doing it now, with no deadlines anywhere near you, is a lot less painful than doing it in week three of term.

1. Put every date you know about in one place

One calendar. Phone, paper, whatever you'll actually look at, but one of them and not three. Right now you probably know your term dates and not much else, which is fine. Add what you've got:

  • Term start, half terms, end of term
  • Mock exam windows, if your school has published them (loads publish in September, so leave a gap)
  • Any coursework or NEA deadline you already know about
  • Anything non-school that'll eat a weekend: work shifts, matches, family stuff, birthdays

That last one matters more than it sounds. Timetables tend to die in week two because they were built for a week that doesn't exist. Knowing about the Saturday shift in advance costs you nothing. Finding out about it on Saturday morning is what wrecks the plan.

2. Give paper somewhere to live

One folder or divider per subject. That's it. No colour scheme, no laminating, and you definitely don't need forty quid of stationery to feel like someone who has their life together.

The only rule that matters: there's a default place for a sheet of paper to go when a teacher hands it to you, so it doesn't end up compressed at the bottom of your bag until March. Same idea if you work on a laptop or tablet. One folder per subject, named properly, made today rather than in a panic later.

3. Find out your exam boards and write them down

Highest value fifteen minutes on this list, and loads of people get to Easter without doing it. For each subject you want two things: the exam board, and the specification code. Something like Maths, AQA, 8300. Ask your teacher in the first week, or check your school's website, then put all of them on one page.

It matters because the boards genuinely differ. Set texts, required practicals, case studies and paper structures all vary depending on which one you're sitting, so revision aimed at the wrong board is an afternoon you don't get back. 4. Do one honest subject audit

Take ten minutes and write your subjects into three columns: fine, patchy, and genuinely no idea. Be honest, because nobody's reading it. You'd be surprised how much calmer it feels to have the thing written down instead of vaguely dreading it.

Then pick one topic out of the "no idea" column and note it down. Not to fix this week. Just so that when the first free hour in September turns up, you already know where to point it, instead of defaulting to whichever subject you like most.

5. Pick one default hour and leave the timetable alone

Don't build a full timetable yet. You haven't got enough information, and it'll be wrong by week two anyway. Decide one thing instead: when your default revision hour is. Same slot, most days, on whichever days actually work for you. Tuesday, Wednesday and Thursday after dinner, say.

A default slot survives real life in a way a colour-coded grid doesn't, because there's no decision to make and nothing to feel guilty about missing. Once term starts and you know what your real week looks like, you can build the proper version. Our post on building a revision timetable that actually works walks through that. For now, one slot is plenty.

6. What not to bother with this week

Don't start revising content. Anything you learn in August with no structure around it will be gone by October, and all you'll have achieved is making the year feel longer than it already is.

Don't make a beautiful timetable, don't rewrite last year's notes to look nicer, and don't buy revision guides before you know your board. Also, ignore anything telling you Year 11 is the hardest year of your life. It's a busy year with a lot of admin attached, and admin is what you've just spent two hours defusing.

The whole list, in one place. One calendar with every date you know about. One folder per subject. Exam boards and spec codes written down. A three column subject audit. One default revision hour. Nothing else.

Do that before term starts and you walk into September with the boring stuff already handled, which means the first few weeks can be about settling in rather than catching up. And if you only do one of them, do the exam boards. Fifteen minutes, and it's the one that costs the most to get wrong later.

Enjoy the rest of the holiday. You'll be fine.
