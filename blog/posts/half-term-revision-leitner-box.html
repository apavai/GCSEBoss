GCSE Half-Term Revision: The Box System That Actually Works

Half-term has just started and your Year 11 still has papers next week. Here's the single most evidence-backed revision technique they can use this week — and why almost no one does it properly.

If you're trying to work out how to make GCSE half-term revision count — without your teenager spending the week panic-cramming, or treating it like a holiday and then panicking on Sunday night — there is genuinely a right answer to this. It's been around since 1972. It's free. Almost nobody uses it properly.

It's called the Leitner box, and once you understand it you can't really unsee it. The reason your teenager's revision often feels like running on a treadmill — covering the same topic five times and still not knowing it on the day — is that they're using the wrong system. The Leitner box is the right one.

What the Leitner box actually is

In 1972 a German science journalist called Sebastian Leitner published a book called So lernt man lernen — How to Learn to Learn. In it he described a small system of physical boxes, each one a tray of flashcards. You start with all your cards in Box 1. You quiz yourself. The cards you get right move to Box 2. The cards you get wrong stay in Box 1.

Box 1 you review every day. Box 2 you review less often — say, every two or three days. Box 3 less often again. Box 5 you might only look at once a fortnight. When you get a card right in Box 2, it moves to Box 3. When you get a card wrong in Box 5, it goes all the way back to Box 1.

That's the whole system. It looks too simple to matter. It's the closest thing to a free win in GCSE revision that exists.

Why it works — the bit the textbooks don't explain

The Leitner box quietly forces a teenager to do two things at once that the evidence on learning is unambiguous about. The first is testing themselves rather than re-reading the material. The second is spacing that testing out over time rather than cramming it.

In 2013 a team led by John Dunlosky at Kent State University published a review in Psychological Science in the Public Interest of every common student study technique. They looked at ten of them. Only two scored "high utility": practice testing, and distributed practice. The Leitner box is both, in one system. Highlighting, re-reading, and summarising — the three things teenagers default to in half-term — all scored "low".

Put another way: most of what your Year 11 is probably doing this week is, in measured terms, the least effective thing they could be doing. The Leitner box swaps them onto the highest-evidence technique we have, and does it without a lecture.

How to set one up this week

It needs to be a physical box. Apps exist, and they work, but for most teenagers the physical movement of the card — picking it up, getting it right, sliding it into the next box — is the bit that makes the system stick. The friction of moving a card by hand is the point.

You need: a small plastic divider box (a few quid from anywhere that sells stationery), five sections labelled 1–5, and a stack of plain index cards. That's it. The whole setup is under £10.

Help them seed Box 1 with twenty cards on a topic they're shaky on. Just twenty to start with. One concept per card — question on the front, answer on the back. The hand-writing is part of the work. They'll add more once they see the system actually doing something.

The GCSE half-term revision rhythm — day by day

Box 1 gets reviewed every day this week — fifteen minutes, no more. Cards they get right move to Box 2. Cards they get wrong stay where they are.

Box 2 gets reviewed every other day. Box 3 every fourth day. Box 4 weekly. Box 5 every fortnight. Don't agonise about the intervals. Anything in the right direction — that is, each box reviewed less often than the box before it — gets you most of the gain. The specific intervals are a tuning question, not a make-or-break one.

Fifteen focused minutes on Box 1 every day in half-term beats two hours of re-reading on Wednesday. Defending those fifteen minutes — that's the parent's job this week.

Three things parents can actually do

First, buy the box. Don't ask them if they want one. Put it on their desk on Monday morning with a stack of index cards. The friction of starting is the biggest reason teenagers don't adopt new revision systems. Removing it is the first job.

Second, help them seed it. Sit down with them for half an hour on the first day and help them write the first twenty cards on whatever topic they're least confident about. Not the topic they like — the one they're avoiding. After the first twenty they'll do the rest themselves. The first twenty is the hard bit.

Third, defend the daily fifteen minutes. Same time each day. Phone in another room. No music with lyrics. Just the box. They will resist this on Wednesday. They will resist it harder on Thursday. By Friday they will be quietly converting cards from Box 1 to Box 2 and telling you nothing about it. That's the moment the system has taken.

Why half-term specifically

Half-term is the right week to start a new revision system because the rest of the exam window is too rushed. Once papers come thick and fast, there's no spare attention to embed a new habit. Half-term has just enough structure-free time to teach a teenager the rhythm without the rhythm being immediately destroyed by a paper at 9am tomorrow.

Start the system this week, and by the second week of June it's running on its own. That's the whole bet.

One last thing

The Leitner box is quiet, slow, and evidence-led. It does not feel dramatic. It does not produce the satisfying ache of a four-hour revision session. It produces something better, which is a teenager who walks into the exam room knowing the difference between the cards they've already mastered and the ones they're still working on. Most teenagers don't have that. The ones who do, do better.

Buy the box. Help them seed it. Defend the fifteen minutes. The rest takes care of itself.
