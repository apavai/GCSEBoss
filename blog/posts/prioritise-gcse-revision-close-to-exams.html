How to Prioritise GCSE Revision Close to Exams: Paper in 3 Days vs Paper in 3 Weeks

Maths Paper 2 is next Wednesday for most boards. English Literature Paper 2 is still nearly three weeks out. Your Year 11 has roughly the same amount of energy left for both. Here's the criterion we'd quietly suggest if you asked us at the school gate.

If you've been working out how to prioritise GCSE revision close to exams, this is the week the question stops being theoretical. We're in Week 4 of an 8-week exam window. Papers start clustering. Some are days away. Others are still a fortnight out. And the conversation around the kitchen table tends to land in the same place every Thursday evening: should they spend the weekend on the paper that's almost here, or the one that's further out but bigger?

The unhelpful version of that conversation is the one where you write the timetable for them. The useful version is the one where you give them a criterion they can apply themselves — once now, and again next week when the next cluster lands. What follows is the criterion most of the parents and teachers we speak to converge on, plus the two or three small adjustments worth making this weekend.

How to prioritise GCSE revision close to exams: nearest paper wins, with one exception

The default for revision priority close to exams is the paper that's closest. The reasoning isn't subtle. Working memory is finite, exam-week fatigue is cumulative, and content gets pushed out of short-term recall surprisingly quickly under tiredness. Whatever they revise on Saturday lands hardest in the paper that sits on Monday or Wednesday. The same study on Saturday for a paper three weeks out is partly going to wash through by the time they sit it.

The exception — and it's a real one — is when the further-out paper is the substantially weaker subject. If they're broadly comfortable with the paper in three days and genuinely shaky on the one in three weeks, the calculus changes. A weak subject needs more total contact time over more days to move; you can't compress that into the week before. Three sessions a week over three weeks on the weaker paper, alongside heavier work on the imminent paper, lands better than a panic week.

How to tell which one is actually the weaker subject

This is the bit parents tend to get wrong — not because they don't know their teenager, but because the signals you see at home are noisy. A teenager who complains loudly about History often does well in it. A teenager who never mentions Chemistry might be quietly drowning. Self-report from a Year 11 in the middle of the exam window is one of the least reliable data sources in the house.

The signal you want is topic-level. Not "are they confident in Maths" — that's too broad to act on — but "of the seven topics on Paper 2, which three have they actually practised in the last fortnight and got mostly right". If you can't answer that question, you don't yet have the data to prioritise. And if your teenager can't answer it either, that's the prior question, not the timetable.

The fix here is small. Ask them to pull up their last two past-paper attempts for each subject and look at where the marks were dropped. Five minutes of that conversation will tell you both more about the right priority than an hour of negotiating which day to revise what.

The three-day vs three-week split — what good practice looks like this weekend

In practice, "nearest paper wins" doesn't mean spending every hour of the weekend on the imminent paper. It means weighting, not exclusivity. A rough split that works for most Year 11s this week, assuming a Maths Paper 2 next Wednesday and an English Literature paper roughly three weeks out:

Saturday morning: two focused hours on the imminent paper — past-paper questions under timed conditions on the specific topic types they've struggled with. Saturday afternoon: rest, ideally outside the house. Saturday evening: optional thirty to forty-five minutes on the further-out paper — but only on something that's genuinely a memory-load task, like quote recall, not on something that needs deep focus.

Sunday morning: another focused block on the imminent paper, this time on the topics they got wrong on Saturday. Sunday afternoon: off again. Sunday evening: one short pass over notes for the further-out paper, then early to bed. That's roughly the shape. The detail is theirs to set. The shape is yours to gently defend.

The trap: revising the easy paper because it's nearer

The single most common pattern we see is the teenager who picks the imminent paper precisely because they're more comfortable with it — and then spends the weekend on the topics they already know. It feels like productivity. It is, technically, revision. But the marginal mark gain on a topic they already mostly know is tiny compared to the marginal gain on a topic they don't.

The corrective question, if you can ask it without it sounding like a test, is something like: "What's the topic on Wednesday's paper you'd least want to come up?" Whatever they say, that's where the weekend's revision should sit. Not the topic they want to do. The topic they want to avoid.

One thing to actually decide together

Sleep schedule for Tuesday night. That's the only piece of the week that benefits from being a joint decision, because it benefits from being a deadline rather than a target. Lights-out by 10:30pm the night before a paper, ideally 10. Most teenagers won't choose this for themselves; most parents under-defend it. Pre-committing on Sunday evening to a Tuesday-night cut-off is the highest-leverage conversation you have this week.

Everything else — what to revise, how, in what order — is theirs. The criterion above is yours to share if asked, not yours to enforce. The job description for the next ten days is to keep the kitchen normal, the fridge stocked, and the schedule defendable.

Where the parent dashboard quietly helps

One of the reasons we built the parent dashboard inside GCSE Boss the way we did — subject coverage by topic, not just by subject — is precisely this week's question. The "which paper is the bigger weakness" call gets a lot easier when you can see, at topic level, what's been covered and where the recent practice attempts have landed. Real visibility, without the drama. You see what's happening without having to ask every evening.

It doesn't decide the priority for you. The criterion above still applies. But it removes the noisy self-report layer, which is the layer that makes the kitchen-table version of this conversation so difficult.

Three weeks more

Most boards finish their exam window in the week of 22 June. From this Thursday, that's about four working weeks of papers left, with one half-term-style breather still inside the window for some teenagers. Your job is small and very specific from here: ask the criterion question once if it's useful, defend the Tuesday-night sleep, and otherwise let them set the shape.

The paper in three days will be fine. So will the one in three weeks. The thing that determines how both land is mostly whether they slept on Tuesday, ate something on Wednesday morning, and arrived at the desk with the topics they were actually shaky on already practised. Everything else is texture.
