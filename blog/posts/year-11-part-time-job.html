Year 11 Part-Time Job: How to Agree Hours That Leave Room for School

A Year 11 part-time job can be a real boost. It brings money of their own, confidence with adults and a break from screens. It can also quietly squeeze the time that GCSE year needs. This guide helps you and your teenager agree hours together, so the job fits around school rather than the other way round.

Autumn is when the question tends to come up. Shops and cafés start recruiting for the busy season, friends start talking about their shifts, and your teenager starts asking whether they can have a go too. At the same time, mock letters are landing and homework is building. A year 11 part time job can be good for them, and it needs a plan.

Start with the law, then your family

Before you talk about hours, check what the law allows. Rules on children's work cover things like when young people can work, for how long, and which jobs are allowed. Your local council applies those rules and can tell you whether it needs to know about the job.

The law in this area has recently been updated, and not every change is in force yet. That means a figure you read on a forum or hear from another parent may already be out of date. The safest place to check is your council's child employment page. Search for your council's name plus "child employment", read the current guidance, and keep the link handy.

Employers have a duty to follow these rules too. A good employer will ask about school and will not push a student to work beyond what is allowed. If an employer seems vague about the rules, treat that as useful information.

Once you know the legal edges, the real conversation is about your family and your teenager.

How many hours should a Year 11 work?

There is no single right answer, and it is not a number you should borrow from someone else's household. Within the law, how many hours a Year 11 works is a family decision. It depends on your teenager, their school week and what else they care about.

A few questions help you get there together:

  • What does a normal week already hold? School, travel, homework, clubs, sport, caring responsibilities and time with friends all count.
  • How much sleep do they need to feel like themselves? Late finishes followed by early starts add up quickly.
  • When are the mocks and any coursework deadlines? Those weeks may need a lighter plan.
  • How do they feel at the end of a school day? Some teenagers come home buzzing; others need quiet time to recover.
  • Why do they want the job? Saving for something, independence and wanting to try work all lead to slightly different plans.

The answers usually point to a sensible amount of work far more clearly than a rule of thumb ever could.

A Year 11 part-time job plan you agree together

The plan works best when your teenager leads and you ask questions. Sit down with a paper calendar or a shared phone calendar and try this order.

  1. Put the fixed things in first. School, travel, regular homework time, clubs and family commitments go on the calendar before anything else.
  2. Protect sleep. Mark a sensible bedtime on school nights and treat it as part of the plan, not an extra.
  3. Add the mock and deadline weeks. Your teenager can find these on the school letter or the school website.
  4. Now look at where a shift could fit. Keep it inside what the law allows, and leave some free time around it. A week with no breathing space rarely lasts.
  5. Agree a review. Choose a point a few weeks in, and another just after mocks, when you both sit down and ask how it is going.

Writing it down turns a vague "don't let it affect your schoolwork" into something you can both check calmly.

Protect the weeks that matter

Most employers of students expect requests for time off around exams. The trick is asking early. Encourage your teenager to tell their manager about mock dates as soon as they know them, and to put the request in writing if the workplace uses an app or a rota system.

It is also worth agreeing in advance what happens if a busy week at work collides with a busy week at school. For most families, school wins that tie. Settling it calmly now saves a tense conversation later.

Warning signs worth noticing

Even a well-planned job can drift. Signs that the balance needs a look include:

  • Tiredness that does not lift at weekends.
  • Homework being rushed or missed.
  • Dropping clubs, sport or friends they used to enjoy.
  • Dreading shifts, or seeming worried after work.
  • Being asked to stay late or cover extra shifts at short notice, often.

If you notice any of these, start with curiosity rather than blame. Ask what is going on, and look at the plan together. Often a small change fixes it: a shift moved, a lighter week, a clearer conversation with the manager. Stopping the job is an option, but it does not have to be the first one.

Part-time job during GCSEs: keeping them safe

A first job is also a first step into a world of adults, rotas and responsibilities. A little groundwork helps.

Know where they work, who their manager is and how they get home, especially after dark. Make sure they know they can say no to tasks that feel unsafe, and that they should never be pressured into working longer than agreed. Encourage them to keep their payslips and rotas, which helps if anything is ever unclear.

Most of all, make sure they have a trusted adult to talk to if something at work does not feel right. That might be you, another family member or a teacher. If they would rather talk to someone outside the family, Childline is free and confidential, by phone or online. If you are worried an employer is not following the rules, your council's child employment team is the place to raise it.

What a Year 11 part-time job gives back

It is easy to focus on the risks, so it is worth naming the upside too. A job can teach time management in a way no lecture from a parent can. It builds confidence talking to adults and customers. It shows reliability, which can be useful for a sixth form, college or apprenticeship application. And it gives your teenager a real sense of what money costs to earn.

If shorter revision sessions become part of the week, GCSE mock revision in short blocks is the same idea: focused practice that fits between other commitments.

The short version

Check the rules on your council's child employment page. Agree the hours together as a family, inside the law. Protect sleep and the weeks that matter. Review the plan after a few weeks and again after mocks. A job that fits around school can be one of the most useful things your teenager does this year.
