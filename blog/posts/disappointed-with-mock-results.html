Disappointed With Mock Results? What to Say to Your Teenager First

If your teenager is disappointed with mock results, the first conversation matters more than any revision plan you could draw up that evening. A mock paper can land hard. Your child may have worked for it, hoped for more, or simply found the day did not go the way they expected. What they need first is not a solution. They need to feel that you are on their side, that one paper has not changed how you see them, and that there is a sensible way forward they can help to shape.

Mock timing varies from school to school. Some Year 11 students have sat mocks already and have their papers back, some are still waiting, and some schools hold mocks after Christmas. This guide is for whenever results come back in your house, whether that is this week or later in the year.

Start by listening, not fixing

When a teenager walks in with a disappointing paper, it is natural to want to fix it straight away. You might reach for a timetable, a list of topics or a promise of extra help. Hold that thought for now.

Start with something simple and open, such as:

  • "How are you feeling about it?"
  • "Do you want to talk about it now, or later?"
  • "What was the paper like on the day?"

Then let them answer. Some teenagers want to talk at length. Others say very little at first and come back to it at bedtime or in the car. Both are fine. If they are not ready, you can say, "That's OK. I'm here when you want to go through it," and mean it.

While they talk, try not to interrupt with advice. Nodding, asking "And then what happened?" and repeating back what you heard all show that you are listening. Feeling heard often takes the heat out of the moment.

What to avoid saying in the first conversation

A few common phrases can make a disappointed teenager feel worse, even when they are meant kindly:

  • Comparisons. Mentioning a sibling, a cousin or a friend's result turns a private setback into a contest.
  • "I told you so." If revision was a source of tension before the mock, this is not the moment to replay it. Our guide to arguments about GCSE revision has ideas for that conversation on a calmer day.
  • Instant plans. "Right, from tomorrow you're doing two hours a night" can feel like a punishment rather than support.
  • Brushing it off. "It doesn't matter, it's only a mock" may be meant to reassure, but it can sound as if their feelings do not count.

You do not have to get every word right. If something comes out wrong, a simple "Sorry, that came out badly. I'm on your side" goes a long way.

Keep one paper in proportion

Once the first wave of feeling has passed, it can help to put the result in context. A mock is a practice run. It shows where things stand at one point in Year 11, on one day, under one set of conditions. It is not a prediction of the summer and it is not a verdict on your child's ability.

You might say something like, "This is one paper. It tells us what to work on, and there is still time to work on it." Keep it honest and steady. Avoid promising a particular result later, because no one can promise that, and teenagers usually spot it when we try.

It also helps to separate the result from the person. "That paper was hard" lands very differently from "You did badly." Talking about the paper, the questions and the preparation keeps the focus on things that can change.

Ask what they think went wrong

When your teenager is ready, ask for their view before offering yours. Questions that invite reflection work well:

  • "Which part of the paper felt hardest?"
  • "Was it the topics, the time or the nerves on the day?"
  • "Was anything a surprise?"

Teenagers often have a clearer picture than we expect. They may say they ran out of time, misread a question, or revised the wrong topics. Their answer tells you far more than the mark on its own, and it means the next step comes from them rather than being handed down.

If they say "I don't know," that is fine too. It is an honest answer, and the teacher's feedback can help fill in the gaps.

Use the teacher's feedback

The most useful information after a mock usually comes from school, not from the number at the top of the page. Look together at any written comments, marked questions or targets the teacher has given. If the school has sent a report, our guide to reading Year 11 mock results calmly explains how to make sense of it before you plan anything.

Encourage your teenager to ask their teacher one clear question, such as "What is the one thing I should focus on first?" Teachers know the specification, the paper and your child's work, so their answer is likely to be more useful than a general plan from home.

Agree one next step together

After listening, putting the paper in proportion and looking at the feedback, aim to agree just one next step. One step is much easier to start than a long list, and it builds a sense of control.

That step might be:

  1. Going through the paper with the teacher's comments and correcting two or three questions.
  2. Practising one topic that came up as a gap, little and often.
  3. Trying a timed section at home to get used to the pace.

Let your teenager choose the step where possible, and ask how you can help. For some, help means quiet space and a cup of tea. For others, it means a lift to the library or someone to test them on key terms. If your child has written a short note about how the mock went, as suggested in our student guide to what to do after mock exams, that note can be a good starting point.

Then step back. Check in after a week or so, gently, rather than every evening.

When feeling disappointed with mock results does not lift

Feeling low for a day or two after a disappointing result is normal. If your teenager seems upset for longer, stops enjoying things they usually like, struggles to sleep or eat, or starts avoiding school, it is worth speaking to someone. Their form tutor, head of year or the school's pastoral team will know them and can offer support. Your GP is another good place to start if you are worried about their wellbeing.

You do not need to wait for things to get serious. A quick word with school can make a real difference, and it shows your teenager that asking for help is a normal, sensible thing to do.

The conversation they will remember

Your teenager may not remember the exact mark on a November mock, but they are likely to remember how you responded. Listening first, keeping one paper in proportion and agreeing one small step together tells them something important: a setback is something you face together, and it is not the end of the story.
