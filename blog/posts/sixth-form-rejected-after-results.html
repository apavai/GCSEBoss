Sixth Form Said No? What Your Options Actually Are

A rejection from a sixth form or college feels like a door slamming, but on results day it's almost never the end of the road. Here's the calm version of what happens next, and why there's more room to move than it looks in the first hour.

If you're searching "sixth form rejected my child" in the run-up to results day, take a breath first. This is a much more common, and much more fixable, situation than it feels right now. GCSE results land on Thursday 20 August 2026, and every year a chunk of families discover that a conditional place has fallen through because a grade came in below what the sixth form asked for. It's an unwelcome surprise, but it is not a verdict on your teenager, and it is not the last word on where they'll be in September. There is a route forward from every set of results.

The single most important thing in the first hour is to not make any big decisions in the first hour. Look at the grades, have a cup of tea, and give yourselves permission to work the options out properly rather than in a panic.

First, the thing that's still true

Whatever a single sixth form has decided, your teenager is still entitled to a place in education or training. In England, local authorities run what's called the September Guarantee: a duty to make sure every 16- and 17-year-old is offered a suitable education or training place. On top of that, the law asks young people to stay in some form of education or training until they're 18, but "education or training" is broad: a school or college course, an apprenticeship, or part-time study alongside work or volunteering all count. In other words, the system is built on the assumption that everyone lands somewhere. One "no" from one provider doesn't change that.

"A sixth form rejected my child": the first, calmest step

Before anything else, phone the sixth form or college that said no and ask two plain questions: is the decision final, and is there any flexibility on the grade that fell short? Admissions teams are dealing with this exact conversation all day on results day, and the answer isn't always a flat no. Some will hold a place if a resit is planned, some have a little give on a single grade, and some run a foundation or alternative course that leads to the same place a year on. Ask what they can offer, not just whether the original offer stands. Keep the tone warm and factual: you're gathering information, not making a case yet.

Can you appeal, and is it worth it?

There are two different "appeals" people mix up here. The first is a review of marking on the exam itself: if a grade is close to the boundary and a place depends on it, the school or college that entered your teen can ask the exam board to re-check the marking, often through a priority service that's faster because an offer is riding on it. That request goes through the school's exams officer, so speak to them quickly. The windows are tight. The second is an admission appeal against the sixth form's decision itself. For school sixth forms this usually means an independent panel, and appeals turned down on academic grounds are heard later in September. Be realistic: the panel only asks whether the decision was reasonable, which is a high bar, and these appeals are rarely upheld, so it's wise to line up other options at the same time rather than waiting on it.

Other places are still taking students

This is the option most families underestimate. Plenty of colleges and sixth forms still have spaces after results day, and many run post-results applications that open around 20 August. You can apply fresh once the grades are actual rather than predicted. A-level results come out a week earlier, on Thursday 13 August, which quietly frees up places as students move on, so it's genuinely worth a round of calls and online enquiries to nearby colleges. A course that's a slightly different shape at a college that wants them is often a better year than a reluctant place somewhere that didn't. Our guide to what grades you need for sixth form is a useful reference while you compare.

Apprenticeships and other routes worth a proper look

A rejection is also a natural moment to widen the search rather than narrow it. Apprenticeships combine paid work with study and are a serious, well-regarded route into a lot of careers; T-Levels are two-year technical courses with a big industry placement; and traineeships or supported internships can bridge a gap for teens who aren't quite ready for either. None of these are a consolation prize. For the right young person they fit better than a classroom sixth form ever would. The National Careers Service (0800 100 900) is a free place to talk options through if you want an outside steer. Our round-up of what to do after GCSEs lays the main paths out side by side.

If it came down to English or Maths

Very often the grade that blocked a place is English Language or Maths, because those are the two most post-16 courses ask for at grade 4. If that's your situation, the November resit series exists for exactly this: your teen can sit those papers again just weeks after results day, with the content still fresh, and a pass can reopen doors that felt shut in August. It's worth raising with any college you're talking to, because some will offer a place on the understanding a resit is on the way. We've written a full, plain-English walk-through of GCSE resits in November if that's the road you end up on.

What to say at home this week

The practical steps matter, but so does the weather in the house. A teenager who's just been turned down is often reading it as a judgement on them, and the most useful thing you can do is gently separate the two: the door closing is about a grade and a numbers game, not about who they are or what they're capable of. Skip the "what went wrong" post-mortem for now, skip comparisons with friends and cousins entirely, and hold off on any big-picture decisions until everyone's had a day to settle. "We'll sort this, and there's more than one way through" said calmly, once, does more than a long conversation. If your teen is really struggling (not sleeping, withdrawing, or talking in a way that worries you), it's always reasonable to speak to your GP, and Childline (0800 1111) is free, confidential and available 24/7. Reaching for support early is good parenting, not an overreaction.

Where results come from

One last bit of context to keep the week in proportion: the grades themselves were set by the exam boards and simply passed on by the school on results day.
