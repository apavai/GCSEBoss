GCSE Mock Revision: How to Start When the Course Is Not Finished Yet

GCSE mock revision feels strange in the autumn term, because half the course has not been taught yet. You can still start. The trick is to revise what you have covered, leave space for what is coming, and keep each session small.

A mock letter has probably landed by now, or it will soon. Some schools sit mocks in November or December. Others start earlier, and a few begin in the middle of October. Dates vary a lot from school to school, so the first job for GCSE mock revision is simple: read your own school's letter and put the real dates somewhere you will see them. Do not go by what a friend at another school says.

Once you know your dates, this post walks through a plan that works when the course is still running. It is not a new timetable, just a way to make the time you spend now count.

Why GCSE mock revision feels awkward right now

In May, revision is about the whole course. In late September it is different. You are revising a course that is only partly built.

That creates two common traps. The first is waiting until everything has been taught, which means starting far too late. The second is trying to teach yourself every topic that has not come up in class yet, which is exhausting and usually confusing. Neither is necessary.

It helps to remember what mocks are for. A mock is a practice run. It shows you and your teachers which topics are secure, which need work, and how you handle timed questions. It is useful information, not a final verdict on you. Treat it as a check-up, and the pressure drops a little.

Step one: sort every subject into two lists

Take one sheet of paper per subject. Draw a line down the middle.

On the left, write taught: the topics you have already covered in lessons, including anything from Year 10 that your teacher has said will be in the mock. On the right, write not yet taught: the topics still to come before the mock.

If you are not sure which side something belongs on, ask your teacher. A quick "Which topics will be in the mock?" at the end of a lesson is a normal question, and most teachers are glad to hear it. Many schools also send a topic list with the mock letter, so check there first.

This sheet does three things at once. It shows you how much you already know. It stops you from panicking about topics nobody expects you to know yet. And it gives you a starting point for tonight.

Step two: revise only the taught list for now

Your GCSE mock revision this term should live on the left side of the sheet.

Pick one topic from the taught list. Spend about twenty to thirty minutes on it. That is enough time to do something useful and short enough that you will actually start.

Good things to do in that block:

  • Close the book and write down everything you remember. Then open it and fill the gaps in a different colour. The gaps are your revision list.
  • Answer a few exam-style questions. Your teacher, your textbook or your school's revision pages will have some. Mark them honestly.
  • Make one small set of flashcards. Five to ten cards on the key facts or methods for that topic, no more.

Rereading notes feels productive but fades fast. Pulling information out of your head works better.

Step three: leave a gap for what is still coming

The right side of your sheet is not a to-do list for this week. It is a reminder.

As each new topic is taught in class, move it across to the taught side and add it to your rotation. That way your revision grows with the course instead of racing ahead of it.

If there is a topic on the right that you know will be taught only a week or two before the mock, flag it. Pay extra attention in those lessons, and ask your teacher what the best way to review it quickly will be. You do not need to learn it before it has been taught.

How to fit mock revision around homework

You already have homework, and possibly a club, a job or other commitments. Mock revision should fit around those, not replace sleep.

A realistic start for many Year 11 students is two or three short blocks a week, each on one topic from one subject. Rotate the subjects so nothing is left untouched for weeks. As the mocks get closer, you can add a block or two if it feels manageable.

Some simple ways to protect the blocks:

  • Attach a block to something you already do, such as straight after dinner on set days.
  • Keep your phone in another room for those twenty or thirty minutes.
  • Stop when the block ends, even if you feel you could go on. Finishing on time makes the next one easier to start.

If a week goes badly, do not try to double up the week after. Just pick up the next block as normal.

What to practise in each subject

Every subject is a little different, but the same idea applies. Revise the taught topics, and practise the kind of questions you will actually face.

In maths and the sciences, that usually means working through questions with a method, not just reading worked examples. In English and the humanities, it often means planning answers and writing a paragraph or two under a loose time limit. In languages, it can mean short bursts of vocabulary plus a little speaking or writing practice.

If you are unsure what format the mock questions will take in a subject, ask your teacher. Knowing the format early saves you from practising the wrong thing.

Keep the pressure in proportion

It is normal to feel a bit uneasy when a mock letter arrives. That feeling is a sign you care, not a sign you are behind.

A few reminders that help:

  • Mocks are practice. They show you what to work on next.
  • Your teachers want the mocks to help you. If you are worried, talk to them, your form tutor or someone at home.

If worry about exams starts to affect your sleep, your eating or your mood, tell a trusted adult. That is a normal thing to do, and support is available at school.

A GCSE mock revision starter for tonight

If you want to begin today, here is a five-minute version:

  1. Find your school's mock letter and write down the dates.
  2. Pick your first subject and draw the taught and not yet taught columns.
  3. Choose one taught topic and put a twenty-minute block in your week.

That is enough to start. You can build the rest of the plan one subject at a time over the next few days.

Starting small now gives you time. When the mocks arrive, you will have met most of the material more than once. Good luck, and check that letter.
