How to Talk to Your Teenager About Revision

It's the final week of the exam window, and most evenings end the same way: you ask how revision's going and get a one-word answer. Knowing how to talk to your teenager about revision isn't about the perfect speech, it's one question worth asking, and the nightly one worth dropping.

We talk to a lot of parents at this point in the year, and the worry is almost always the same. You can see the exams stacking up. You want to help. But every attempt to check in seems to land as nagging, and you're left wondering whether you're doing too much or not enough. The good news: the difference between a conversation that helps and one that ends in a sigh usually comes down to a single word change.

Why the nightly "have you done enough?" backfires

"Have you done enough revision today?" feels like a reasonable question. It's also almost impossible for a teenager to answer well. There's no version of "yes" that sounds convincing and no version of "no" that doesn't invite a follow-up. So they shut down, not because they're hiding anything, but because the question is really asking them to defend themselves at the end of a long, tiring day.

In the final fortnight especially, that nightly check-in starts to carry a lot of weight. It turns every evening into a small exam of its own, where the subject is "are you working hard enough?" and the examiner is the person they most want to feel relaxed around. Drop it. Not because you don't care how much they're doing, but because asking it out loud rarely makes them do more, and often makes the house tense for no return.

The one question worth asking instead

Swap it for something forward-looking and concrete: "What's your plan for tomorrow?" Or, if that feels too formal, "What do you want to be on top of by the weekend?" The shift is small but it changes everything. Instead of grading the day that's just gone, you're helping them look at the one ahead, and that's a question a tired teenager can actually answer.

It also does something quietly useful: it hands them the steering wheel. A plan they say out loud is a plan they're more likely to own. You're not telling them to do three hours of chemistry; you're asking what their next move is and trusting them to have one. Most of the time, they do. And when they don't, you've found that out gently, in a way that opens a door rather than closing it.

What to actually do with the answer

Here's the part that's easy to get wrong. When they tell you the plan, the most useful thing you can probably say is almost nothing: "sounds good" and a cup of tea will do more than a critique. The temptation is to improve the plan: shouldn't you do maths before English? what about that topic you struggled with? Resist it. The moment you start editing, you've taken the plan back off them, and the next night they'll have stopped volunteering one.

If you genuinely think something's missing, the lightest possible touch works best: "Is there anything you're avoiding because it's the hard bit?" That's an invitation, not an instruction, and it respects that they know their subjects better than you do. They've been in the lessons all year. Your job in the final week isn't to be a second teacher. It's to be the calm person in the house.

When to say nothing at all

Some evenings the right move is no question at all. If they come downstairs looking wrung out, the conversation they need isn't about revision. It's about anything else. Dinner, a show, the dog, a genuinely terrible joke. Teenagers in the middle of exams are carrying more than they let on, and a house where not every interaction is about work is a real form of support. You're allowed to just be their parent for an hour.

This matters more than it sounds. The aim across the whole final stretch is for home to feel like the place where the pressure goes down, not up. If the only thing waiting for them at the kitchen table is another question about revision, they'll stay in their room. If it's a normal evening with the option to talk, they'll come to you, usually when you least expect it.

The information takes the anxiety out of the question, and that lets you ask the good one ("what's the plan for tomorrow?") from a place of calm rather than worry. Support without hovering is much easier when you're not working in the dark.

How to talk to your teenager about revision: the short version

Knowing how to talk to your teenager about revision in these last days comes down to three small moves: drop the daily "have you done enough?", ask "what's your plan for tomorrow?" instead, and then mostly get out of the way. Add a normal evening or two where revision doesn't come up at all, and a quiet way to see how things are going so you're not guessing. That's it. You don't need the perfect words, you just need to stop asking the question that adds pressure and start asking the one that helps.

None of this means going silent or pretending you're not paying attention. It means picking your moments, trusting them with the plan, and keeping the kitchen table a place they want to sit at. In a week this intense, that calm is worth more than any extra hour you could nag them into.

The exams will be over sooner than it feels right now. Being the steady, unflustered presence at home is one of the most genuinely useful things you can offer in the final week, and it asks far less of you than nagging does.
