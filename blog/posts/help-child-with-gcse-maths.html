How to Help Your Child With GCSE Maths at Home (Even If You Feel Rusty)

If you'd like to help your child with GCSE maths but your own school maths feels a very long time ago, you're not alone. Lots of parents look at a page of algebra and feel that old knot in their stomach. Here's the reassuring part: you don't need to know the methods to be genuinely useful. What helps most at home is interest, routine and a calm space where your child can talk through their thinking.

This guide covers practical ways to help your child with GCSE maths at home, whatever your own confidence with numbers.

Why you can help your child with GCSE maths without being a maths expert

Teachers handle the teaching. Your role is different, and in many ways it's easier. You're there to help your child organise their revision, keep going when a topic feels hard, and notice when they need extra help from school.

Methods also change over time. The way your child is taught to solve a problem may look different from the way you learned it, and that's fine. Trying to teach your own method can sometimes cause confusion, so it often works better to ask them to show you theirs.

If maths wasn't your favourite subject, it's worth keeping that to yourself for now. Saying "I was never any good at maths either" is meant kindly, but it can make it sound as if being "bad at maths" runs in families. A simple "this looks tricky, show me how you're tackling it" keeps the door open.

Ask them to explain it to you

This is the single most useful thing you can do, and it needs no maths knowledge at all. Ask your child to teach you a topic they've been working on. You might say:

  • "Can you show me how you did that one?"
  • "Why does that step work?"
  • "What would you do if the numbers were different?"

Explaining something out loud is one of the best ways to find out whether you really understand it. If your child gets stuck halfway through, that's not a failure. It's useful information about what to look at next. And if you genuinely don't follow, say so. Being "the confused one" gives them the chance to explain more clearly, which helps them too.

Help them find their weak topics

GCSE maths covers a lot of ground, and it's easy for students to keep practising what they already find comfortable. You can help by encouraging a quick, honest check-up.

A simple approach is to sit down together with a list of topics from their teacher or their revision guide and ask them to sort each one into three groups:

  • Confident: I can do these without help.
  • Wobbly: I sort of get it, but I make mistakes.
  • Not yet: I don't really understand this one.

The "wobbly" and "not yet" topics are where revision time pays off most. You don't need to understand the topics yourself. You're just helping them make a plan and stick to it.

If they keep a record of mistakes from homework and tests, that's a brilliant starting point too. Our guide to keeping a GCSE error log explains how it works, and it's something you can easily encourage.

Use school resources and practice papers

Your child's school is the best source of maths practice material. Teachers will often share topic sheets, homework platforms, revision lists and practice papers that match exactly what your child is studying.

It's worth asking your child, or their maths teacher, what's available. Practice papers from your child's school are especially useful later in the year, but even now, short sets of questions on one topic at a time can make a real difference.

If your child is unsure which paper or level they're working towards, encourage them to ask their maths teacher rather than guessing. Our guide to GCSE maths Foundation or Higher can help you both understand the conversation, but their teacher will know their situation best.

Supporting GCSE maths at home: keep it short and regular

Maths revision works best little and often. Twenty to thirty minutes of focused practice a few times a week usually beats one long, exhausting session at the weekend.

Some ideas that help:

  • A regular slot. The same time on the same days makes revision a habit rather than a battle.
  • One topic per session. It's easier to make progress when the goal is small and clear.
  • Do, then check. Encourage them to try questions first, then check answers and look closely at anything they got wrong.
  • Stop on a win. Finishing with a question they can do leaves them more likely to come back next time.

You can help simply by protecting that time: a clear table, phones in another room, and a drink or snack nearby.

When they get frustrated

Maths can be frustrating, especially when a topic just won't click. When that happens, how you respond matters more than any maths tip.

Try to stay calm and curious rather than jumping in with answers. You might suggest a short break, going back to an easier example of the same topic, or writing down exactly where they got stuck so they can ask their teacher. Making a note of the question and taking it into school is a sensible, grown-up move, not a sign of giving up.

Praise effort and good habits rather than results: "I can see you kept going with that" or "nice work sticking to your plan this week". That kind of encouragement helps build confidence over time.

If you notice your child is consistently worried or upset about maths, it's worth getting in touch with their maths teacher or form tutor. They can suggest extra support and will want to know how things are going at home.

GCSE maths help for parents: a quick checklist

  • Ask them to explain a method to you
  • Help them sort topics into confident, wobbly and not yet
  • Point them towards school resources and practice papers
  • Keep sessions short, regular and focused on one topic
  • Stay calm when they're stuck, and praise effort
  • Talk to their teacher if you're worried
