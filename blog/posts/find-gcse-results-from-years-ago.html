How to Find Out GCSE Results From Years Ago (Lost Certificates, Old Grades)

Maybe a job application asks for them. Maybe you're going back into study and someone needs proof. Either way, if you want to find out GCSE results from years ago and you have no idea where the paperwork went, don't panic. There's a clear, well-worn route, and it's more straightforward than it looks.

First, the reassuring bit: your grades don't disappear just because you've lost the certificate. They're on record. Schools, colleges and the exam boards keep them, and there are official ways to get an authorised copy, whether you sat your exams two years ago or twenty. This guide walks through exactly how to find out GCSE results from years ago, in the order that actually saves you time and money.

One thing worth knowing before you start: nobody re-issues your original certificate. The certificate you got on results day is a one-off. What you can get instead is an official, accepted alternative (usually called a certified statement of results), and for most employers and colleges, that does the same job.

How to find out GCSE results from years ago: start with your old school

Your fastest, cheapest first stop is almost always the school or college where you sat the exams (the "exam centre" in official language). Centres keep records of the results they entered students for, and many can print you a statement or confirm your grades directly, sometimes for free or a small admin fee. If the school still exists and you sat your GCSEs reasonably recently, a quick email to the exams officer or main office can sort the whole thing in days rather than weeks.

When you contact them, make it easy to find you: give your full name at the time (including any name you've since changed), your date of birth, the years you attended, and roughly when you sat the exams. The more precise you are, the faster they can pull the record.

Next: go to the exam board

If the school can't help (or you'd rather go straight to source), the exam board that ran your qualifications can issue a certified statement of results. In the UK that means one of the main boards: AQA, Pearson Edexcel, OCR, or WJEC/Eduqas, depending on who set your papers. If you're not sure which board it was, your old school will know, and it's often printed on any paperwork you do still have.

Each board has its own online form and process for requesting a statement. You'll typically need proof of identity and the same details as above: full name at the time, date of birth, the centre you attended, and the year of your exams. The board checks its records and sends back an official document confirming your grades.

When it's trickier: old exams, closed schools and name changes

Not every case is tidy, and that's fine. There's a route for the awkward ones too. If your school has since closed, if you sat your exams a long time ago, or if the exam board itself no longer exists under the same name (boards have merged over the years, and today's boards hold the records of several older ones), the government's official guidance is the place to start untangling it.

GOV.UK publishes a "replacement exam certificates" guidance page that explains who to contact for different situations, including exams taken decades ago and qualifications from boards that have since closed or been absorbed. It points you to whichever current board now holds the historic records. If your search feels like a dead end, that page is how you find the live thread again.

What it costs and how long it takes

Going through your old school is often the cheapest option, sometimes free. Going through an exam board for a certified statement of results usually carries a fee: as a rough guide, expect somewhere around £45 to £50 per statement, though the exact amount varies by board and changes over time, so check the board's current published fee before you pay. Turnaround is typically a couple of weeks. Plan for roughly two to four weeks rather than same-day.

If there's a deadline attached (a job offer, a course enrolment, a visa application), build in that lead time. It's far less stressful to request the statement the moment you know you'll need it than to be chasing it the week it's due.

Do you need the certificate, or just the grades?

It's worth pausing on what the person asking actually wants. Sometimes an employer simply needs to confirm you have, say, a grade 4 (or an old grade C) in English and maths, and a certified statement of results, or even your school confirming the grades, is enough. Other times you need a formal document for an official process. Ask exactly what format they'll accept before you pay for anything, because it can save you a fee and a fortnight.

A quick checklist before you start

Before you send a single email, gather the basics so you only have to ask once: your full name at the time of the exams (plus any later name change), your date of birth, the name of the school or college and the years you attended, and roughly when you sat your GCSEs. Then work the order that's cheapest first: old school or college, then the exam board for a certified statement, and GOV.UK's guidance if the case is older or more tangled. If this year's leavers are reading along, note that fresh 2026 results land on Thursday 20 August 2026, but the routes above are the ones you'll use for anything older.

That's genuinely the whole map. Your results exist, they're recoverable, and the worst case is a short wait and a small fee, not a lost record. Start with the school, keep your details handy, and you'll have your grades in hand sooner than you think.
