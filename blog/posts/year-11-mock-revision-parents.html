Year 11 Mock Revision Parents Can Support: Helping Without Taking Over the Work

Year 11 mock revision parents can help with is often a puzzle: you want to support, and then you are not quite sure how. The letter comes home, the dates go on the fridge, and suddenly there is a question in the air about how much to step in. The short answer is: less than you might think, but more usefully than you might fear. Your job is not to teach the course again. It is to help your child plan, keep things calm, and stay on their side while the work stays theirs.

Mock dates vary a lot between schools. Some run in November, some in December, and some in January. So the first step is not a revision plan. It is the school's own letter.

Start with the school letter, together

Sit down with your teenager and read whatever the school has sent. If nothing has arrived yet, ask them what they have heard in lessons, or check the school's website or parent app. You are looking for three things:

  • When. The dates or the week the mocks start.
  • What. Which subjects, and whether every paper is sat or only some.
  • How. Whether the mocks run in the exam hall or in normal lessons.

Write it down somewhere you can both see. Let your child do the writing if they want to. This small step often lowers the temperature, because a vague worry about "the mocks" becomes a short list of real dates.

Year 11 mock revision parents can actually support

Once you know the dates, the most useful thing you can offer is structure, not content. Most Year 11 students already have plenty of material from school. What they often lack is a clear, short, regular window to use it.

Agree a daily window together. Twenty to forty minutes on school nights is plenty for many students at this stage, with a little more at weekends if they want it. The exact length matters less than the habit. A short window that happens most days beats a long one that never starts.

Let your child choose the subjects for each session. They know which topics feel shaky. If they are stuck, a good default is to start with whichever paper comes first on the mock timetable.

How to help Year 11 with mocks without taking over

This is where many parents feel the pull to do more. A few ways to help that keep the work in your child's hands:

  • Quiz from their notes, not yours. Ask them to hand you a page or a set of flashcards, then ask the questions on it. You do not need to know the answers. You only need to read them out.
  • Ask, don't tell. "Which topic are you doing tonight?" works better than "You should do maths."
  • Keep the space ready. A clear table, a charged laptop if they need one, a glass of water. Small things remove small excuses.
  • Protect the end of the window. When the time is up, let them stop. Stopping on time builds trust that the next session will also be short.
  • Notice effort. "You stuck with that" lands better than any comment about results.

What to avoid: re-teaching topics in a different way from the teacher, setting extra work they did not agree to, or checking every page. These are well meant, but they can turn revision into a negotiation with you rather than a habit of their own.

Supporting Year 11 mock exams on the day

When the mocks arrive, the practical side matters most. Check the timetable together the evening before. Make sure the right equipment is in the bag: black pens, a pencil, a ruler, a calculator if needed, and a clear water bottle if the school allows one. Encourage a normal bedtime rather than a late-night cram.

On the morning, keep things ordinary. Breakfast, a calm "good luck", and no last-minute quizzing at the door. Afterwards, a simple "how did it go?" is enough. Some teenagers want to talk it through. Others want to forget it until tomorrow. Both are fine.

Mocks are practice

It helps to say this out loud, to yourself and to your child: mocks are practice. They exist so students can feel what the real exams are like, find out which topics need more work, and get feedback from teachers while there is still plenty of time to act on it. A mock that goes badly in one subject is useful information, not a verdict.

That framing matters more than any revision technique. A teenager who sees mocks as a chance to learn tends to revise more calmly and recover from a hard paper more quickly.

If your child is anxious or seems to be struggling more than usual, talk to their form tutor or head of year. Schools would much rather hear early, and they can often adjust support quietly.

When the results come back

Mock results usually come back a few weeks later, often with teacher comments. When they arrive, start with the comments rather than the marks. Ask your child which one or two things the teacher suggested, and help them turn those into the next short revision sessions. If they want to practise further, past papers from the school or from their own exam board's website are a good next step. Your child's teacher can tell them which ones match their course. GCSE Boss is not affiliated with any exam board.

A short checklist for this week

  • Read the school's mock letter or timetable together.
  • Write the dates somewhere you both can see.
  • Agree a short daily window, and let your child pick the subjects.
  • Offer to quiz from their notes.
  • Keep the evening before each mock calm and early.
  • Talk to the form tutor if worry seems to be building.

You do not need to become a teacher this term. Steady support, a calm house and a short daily habit are what help most. The work stays theirs, and that is exactly how it should be.
