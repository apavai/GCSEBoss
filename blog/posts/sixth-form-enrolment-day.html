Sixth Form Enrolment: What Happens on the Day and What to Send Them With

The results are in, the appointment letter says Tuesday at eleven, and nobody in the house is entirely sure what the appointment is for. Here is the short version: it is mostly admin, it takes less time than you think, and there is one part of it worth preparing for.

If you are trying to work out what sixth form enrolment actually involves this week, you are in the majority. Most colleges and sixth forms run enrolment across the few days immediately after results day, which puts a lot of families in the same position: a slot booked at short notice, a vague list of things to bring, and a teenager who has been told it is important without being told what happens. The good news is that the day itself is rarely dramatic. The paperwork is quick. What matters is that they walk in with the right documents and walk out having had one useful conversation about their course.

This is a practical guide rather than a reassuring one, although it is also reassuring. What to send them with, what happens in the room, the two questions worth asking, and what can still be changed once the forms are signed.

What sixth form enrolment day actually is

Enrolment is the point where a conditional offer becomes a place. Your teenager applied months ago, got an offer that depended on grades, and enrolment is the college checking the grades, confirming the course, and registering them as a student. In most institutions it runs as a short appointment: a queue, a desk, a member of staff with a screen, and a signature. Twenty minutes is typical. An hour if it is busy or if the conversation about subjects turns out to need having.

It is worth saying plainly that enrolment is not another interview. Nobody is deciding whether they are good enough on the day. That decision was made in the spring and confirmed on results day. The staff in the room are trying to get them onto the right course and out of the door, which is a much friendlier objective than the one most teenagers are braced for.

What to send them with

The list is short, but the day goes badly when one item is missing, because the fix is usually a trip home. Send them with:

  • The printed statement of results from their school. Not a photograph of it, and not a screenshot of the results app. The paper copy, in a folder.
  • The offer or appointment letter from the college, including any reference number on it.
  • Photo ID if they have it, plus a passport or birth certificate if the letter asked for proof of identity or right to study.
  • Proof of address, if requested. A recent utility bill or council tax letter usually does it.
  • Bank details or a parent contact form, if the college handles bursary or travel funding at enrolment. Many do.
  • A pen. Two, ideally, because they will lose one.

If they are going alone, take a photo of the whole set before they leave. It costs thirty seconds and it means that a phone call from the queue asking which grade they got in geography has an answer.

What happens in the room

Broadly, three things. First, someone checks the results against the entry requirements for the course they were offered. Second, they confirm or adjust the subject choices, which is the only genuinely decisional part of the appointment. Third, they do the administrative wrap: student ID photo, timetable or induction date, travel and bursary forms, sometimes a laptop or resources agreement.

The subject conversation is where a bit of preparation pays off. If the grades came in exactly as expected, this takes ninety seconds and nothing changes. If a grade landed above or below the offer, staff will usually raise it and suggest an alternative, and they will suggest it quickly because there is a queue behind them. That is the moment a teenager who has not thought about it says yes to something they have not really considered.

The two questions worth asking on the day

Almost everything else can be looked up later. These two cannot, or at least are much easier to ask in person.

One: what is the deadline for changing a subject, and who do I speak to? Every institution has a change window, and it is almost always longer than students assume (often the first two weeks of term). Getting the date and the name of the person now turns a future panic into an email.

Two: if a grade is being reviewed, does that affect my place? Reviews of marking and access to scripts can be requested from results day, and this year the standard deadline for a review of marking is 24 September 2026. If your teenager is in that queue, the college needs to know today, and you need to know what happens to the place if the grade moves.

What can still be changed afterwards

More than most families expect. A signed enrolment form is not a contract for two years. Subject swaps in the first fortnight of term are common and unremarkable, and colleges plan for them. Course level can sometimes be adjusted in the first few weeks. Bursary and travel applications can usually be submitted after enrolment if a document is missing on the day.

What is harder to change later is the institution itself, simply because places fill. So if your teenager comes home genuinely unhappy about where they have enrolled rather than what they have enrolled onto, that is the conversation to have this week rather than in October.

Your job on the day

Smaller than it feels. Get the folder packed the night before, be reachable by phone, and resist the urge to answer for them at the desk if you go along. Most colleges are happy for a parent to attend, and there is a version of that which helps (you remember the reference number) and a version which does not (they never speak).

Then, when they get back, ask what they signed up for rather than how it went. "How did it go" gets you "fine". "What subjects are you actually on" gets you the information you need, and occasionally reveals that something changed in the room that nobody has mentioned yet. That is not a crisis. It is just the sort of thing that is much easier to sort out in the last week of August than in the third week of September.
