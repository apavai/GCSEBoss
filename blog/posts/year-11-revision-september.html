Year 11 Revision in September: How Much to Expect

September in Year 11 looks louder from the hallway than it is on the page. The useful amount of revision this month is smaller than the speeches.

Year 11 revision in September is not a two-year countdown. It is the work the school actually set this week, plus a short look-back if they have time. Some pupils have had three days of lessons. Some are still catching a staggered start. None of that means you should launch a colour-coded mock timetable tonight.

Load varies by school and by subject. A science teacher who sets two questions is not the same as an English teacher who sets reading. Treat the planner as the source, not a national script and not a neighbour's screenshot.

What year 11 revision in September actually is

Homework is the piece from today's lesson. Revision is going back over something they have already been taught, on purpose, without a due date from this afternoon.

In September those two get mixed up. Someone hears "Year 11" and opens last year's notes. The sheet in the bag does not get done. That is not extra effort. It is avoiding the actual task.

For this month, the order is:

  1. What was set, written down, with a due date
  2. One sitting per piece, timed
  3. Only then a short look-back on a topic they already met in class

If they have not sat in the lesson yet, they do not have revision from that subject. Wait. You are not behind a national clock.

Mocks are not this week's job. May is not this week's job. The first fortnight is about leaving the room with the task, doing it once, and bringing it back. Yesterday's student piece was that. Today is the parent version of the same month: how much extra to add on top, which is less than it feels.

How much to expect on a school night

A reasonable default, unless that teacher said otherwise, is one sitting. Twenty to forty minutes for the piece that is actually due. If three subjects landed on the same night, that is three sittings, not a five-hour block. Eat first. Then the due-soonest one. Park the rest for tomorrow.

Two hours on one worksheet in week one is not grit. It is a missing question, or they are revising the whole topic instead of doing the piece. A stuck sentence for the next lesson is useful. A blank page at 10pm is not.

You do not need to sit next to them. You do need a surface that is not a bedroom floor, a timer they will actually start, and one check that the due piece is in the bag in the morning.

If a club, fixture or job means they get in late, the short piece tonight and the longer one tomorrow is a plan. Write it down so nobody invents it at 10pm.

Phone rules vary by school this term. If the phone stayed in a bag or a locker all day, the planner line is the record. Do not reconstruct the question from a group chat.

Weekends in September

A weekend in the first fortnight is recovery plus whatever is actually due on Monday. It is not a six-hour "get ahead" session.

If they have a piece due, do that. If they do not, one short look-back on a single topic they already met is plenty: twenty minutes, one subject, then stop. Colour-coding every subject is not revision. Buying a new stack of guides before they know which topics this teacher started with is not revision either.

Sleep on Sunday night still beats an extra worksheet invented at home. They will sit in lessons on Monday. That is the job.

What you can leave until later in the autumn

A full exam-year timetable can wait. They do not yet know the pace of this teacher, which topics will be emphasised, or when the first real assessment lands.

A speech about how this year matters more can wait. They have heard it. It does not tell them what to open tonight.

A comparison with a sibling, a neighbour, or last year's cohort can wait. Different school, different set, different start date this week.

November resits are a post-16 job for people who already sat. Current Year 11 are aiming at summer 2027. Do not import that calendar into this month's evening. If you need the post-16 version, it is here.

What actually helps at home

Keep the kit boring. Planner or phone note with due dates. Timer. The book in the bag, not on the floor. Kitchen table is fine. A new desk is not a September requirement.

One check-in, not a running commentary. Ten minutes later in the evening: what was set, what is due, what they are stuck on. Then stop. A hallway quiz on every subject is yesterday's doorstep leaking into Thursday.

If they say "fine" and the planner is empty, that might be true this week. Forms and logins still count as homework. Do not score the month by how many pages they produced on day three.

If they are stuck, the move is "write the last line you understood" and take it in tomorrow, not a two-hour reconstruction with you guessing the method.

Food when they walk in still matters more than an extra topic. A skipped meal plus a blank worksheet is a bad combination. It is not a character test.

The test for this month

You will know September is working if three things are true most weeks:

  • The due piece left the house in the bag
  • Evenings ended at a normal time
  • They can say, in one sentence, what tomorrow's lesson is asking them to bring

That is year 11 revision in September. It is not a mock season. It is not a verdict on the next twenty months. It is homework, one sitting, and enough sleep to sit in the next lesson.

When the school actually names an assessment, you can add a proper revision slot. Until then, the planner is the plan.

That is enough for this month.
