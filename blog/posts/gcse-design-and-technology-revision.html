GCSE Design and Technology Revision: Choosing Materials for a Purpose

A lot of GCSE design and technology revision ends up as long lists of materials and their properties. Knowing those properties matters, but the written paper usually asks for something more: why a particular material suits a particular product. This guide shows you how to practise that skill, so you can link a property to a purpose and explain your choice clearly in an answer.

Mock timing varies from school to school. Some of you have sat your D&T mock already, some are in the middle of mocks, and some have them later in the year. This approach works whenever your next written paper or practice question comes along.

Start with your own specification

Before anything else, check which materials your course actually covers. Different specifications group materials in different ways and expect different levels of detail. Your teacher, your class notes and your specification document are the right sources for what is in scope for you.

If you have not looked at your specification in a while, our guide to revising from your exam specification explains how to use it as a checklist. For materials, that might mean a short list under headings such as papers and boards, timbers, metals, polymers and textiles, depending on your course.

The aim of this post is not to give you another fact bank. It is to show you how to use the facts you already have.

Why "it's strong" is rarely enough

Imagine a question asks why a material has been chosen for a product. A quick answer might be "because it's strong" or "because it's cheap". Those words might be true, but on their own they do not show much understanding.

A fuller answer does three things:

  1. Names a property. For example, a material might be lightweight, stiff, water resistant, easy to cut or easy to print on.
  2. Links that property to the product's purpose. What does the product need to do?
  3. Links it to the user or the context. Who uses the product, and where?

Put together, it might sound like this: "Card is suitable because it is lightweight and easy to print on, so the packaging can show clear product information while keeping postage costs down for the retailer." That is one sentence, but it connects the property to the purpose and the user.

Practise thinking about purpose and user

Before you pick a material, ask yourself two questions about the product.

What does it need to do? A shelf has to hold weight without bending. A food box might need to keep its shape when stacked. A child's toy needs to be safe to handle. The purpose tells you which properties matter most.

Who is it for, and where will it be used? A product used outdoors faces rain and sunlight. A product for young children needs to avoid sharp edges. A product made in large numbers might need a material that is quick and affordable to shape.

Once you have answered both, choosing and justifying a material becomes much easier. You are no longer guessing. You are matching properties to needs.

Compare two materials side by side

One of the most useful ways to practise is to compare two materials for the same product. Pick something simple, such as a desk tidy, a phone stand or a takeaway box, and choose two materials from your specification.

For each one, jot down:

  • two properties that would help the product;
  • one property that might cause a problem;
  • one point about the user or context.

Then decide which material you would choose and write two or three sentences explaining why. This kind of comparison mirrors the reasoning many written questions ask for, and it helps you see that there is often no single right material, only a better justified choice.

You do not need real samples to do this. A notebook and a list of everyday products is enough.

Read the command word first

Materials questions can ask you to state, describe, explain, evaluate or justify. Each one expects a different depth of answer. "State" might need a single property. "Explain" usually needs a property plus a reason. "Justify" or "evaluate" often wants you to weigh up advantages and drawbacks and reach a conclusion.

Underline the command word before you start writing. Our guide to GCSE command words has more on what each one usually asks for.

A simple answer pattern you can practise

When a question asks you to explain or justify a material choice, this pattern can help you structure your thinking:

  1. Property: name one specific property.
  2. Because: say why that property matters for this product.
  3. So: say what that means for the user or the context.

For example: "Pine is used because it is fairly easy to cut and join, so a small workshop can make the frame quickly, which keeps the product affordable for customers."

For longer answers, repeat the pattern for a second property, then add a drawback and a short conclusion. Treat the pattern as a starting point, not a script. Your teacher may prefer a particular structure, so follow their advice.

Check your answers against a mark scheme

Once you have practised a few answers, compare them with a mark scheme or model answer from your teacher. Look at what earns credit. Do the strongest answers link properties to purpose? Do they mention the user? Do they use precise vocabulary?

If you are new to marking your own work, our guide to using a mark scheme for GCSE walks you through it. Keep a short note of anything you missed so you can practise it next time.

Common slips to watch for

A few habits tend to cost clarity in materials answers:

  • Vague words. "Good", "nice" and "strong" need backing up with a specific property and a reason.
  • Listing without linking. Naming four properties without saying why they matter is less useful than explaining one or two well.
  • Forgetting the product. Every point should come back to the product in the question, not materials in general.
  • Ignoring drawbacks. When a question asks you to evaluate, a balanced answer usually mentions a limitation too.

None of these mean you are doing badly. They are easy to fix once you spot them.

Build GCSE design and technology revision into your week

You do not need long sessions to improve. Try ten to fifteen minutes, two or three times a week:

  1. Pick one everyday product.
  2. Choose two materials from your specification.
  3. Write a short comparison using the property, because, so pattern.
  4. Check it against a model answer or ask your teacher for feedback.

Over a few weeks, this kind of GCSE design and technology revision builds the habit of linking materials to purpose, which you can use across many different questions.

If you are unsure about anything on your course, ask your D&T teacher. They know your specification and can point you to the right practice questions.
