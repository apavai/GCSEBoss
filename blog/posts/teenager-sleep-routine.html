Teenager Sleep Routine: Helping Year 11 Build School-Night Habits That Stick

A good teenager sleep routine is one of the most useful things you can help with in Year 11, and one of the hardest to talk about without it turning into a row. By October the novelty of the new school year has worn off, the evenings are getting darker and the clocks go back on Sunday 25 October. If your teenager is coming home shattered, or still scrolling at midnight, you're not alone, and there's a lot you can do that doesn't involve nagging.

This guide is about building school-night habits with your teenager rather than imposing them. The short version of a teenager sleep routine: a regular wind-down, phones charging outside the bedroom by agreement, a steady wake time and a bed that's for sleeping, not revising.

How much sleep does a teenager need?

The honest answer is that it varies from person to person, and guidance is a range rather than a single number. As a general guide:

  • Most teenagers need 8 to 10 hours of sleep each night.

Source: NHS Healthier Together

For more detail, the NHS website has general guidance on sleep for teenagers, and it's a good place to start if you want to read more. What matters day to day is whether your teenager seems rested, can get up without a battle most mornings and has the energy for school and the things they enjoy.

Why Year 11 can feel more tiring

If your teenager seems more tired after school this year, there are some very ordinary reasons. Year 11 often brings longer days, more homework, coursework deadlines and, for some, clubs, sport or a part-time job. Teenagers also tend to feel sleepy later in the evening than they did when they were younger, which makes early nights harder than they sound.

Add darker evenings and a phone full of group chats, and it's easy for bedtime to drift later and later without anyone really deciding it should. None of this is anyone's fault. It just means a routine needs a bit of deliberate setting up.

Building a teenager sleep routine together

The routines that stick are the ones your teenager has a say in. Pick a calm moment, not 11pm on a school night, and talk it through as a joint project. Ask what they think gets in the way of sleep, and what they'd be willing to try for a couple of weeks.

  1. Agree a steady wake time. A consistent wake time is often easier to stick to than a strict bedtime, and it gives the rest of the routine an anchor. Try to keep it roughly the same on weekends where possible, rather than a very long lie-in that makes Sunday night harder.
  2. Build a short wind-down. Thirty minutes or so of something calm before bed helps the day come to a close. Reading, a shower, tidying tomorrow's bag or listening to music all work. The point is that it's the same most nights, so it starts to feel like a signal.
  3. Phones charging outside the bedroom, by agreement. This is usually the big one, and it works best as a household arrangement rather than a punishment. A charging spot in the kitchen or on the landing, used by everyone, feels fairer than a rule just for them. If they need an alarm, a cheap alarm clock solves the "but I need my phone to wake up" problem.
  4. Keep revision out of bed. Revising in bed makes it harder to switch off, and the bed starts to feel like a desk. Encourage a clear stopping point for homework in the evening, done somewhere else, with the bag packed afterwards.
  5. Keep the bedroom calm. A cool, dark and quiet room helps. Small changes like a blackout blind, a lamp instead of the big light in the evening or a tidy-up of the clutter by the bed can make it feel more restful.
  6. Notice caffeine late in the day. Coffee, some fizzy drinks and energy drinks all contain caffeine. It's worth agreeing that drinks later in the evening are caffeine-free, and having something else in the fridge makes that easier.

Teenager sleep on school nights: getting through the clocks change

When the clocks go back, there's an extra hour on the Sunday morning, which sounds like a gift. In practice, the earlier darkness and the shift in timing can unsettle a routine for a few days. Keeping the wake time steady through that week, and getting some daylight in the morning where you can, helps everything settle back down.

Half term is also coming. It's fine to relax things a little, but keeping the wake time within an hour or so of the usual one makes the first Monday back far less painful for everyone.

Talking about it without a battle

Sleep can become a flashpoint because it feels personal. A few things help keep it calm.

Talk about how they feel rather than what time it is. "You've seemed wiped out this week, how are you finding it?" lands better than "you were on your phone until one again". Ask questions rather than giving verdicts, and let them suggest changes too.

Model it yourself where you can. If the whole household winds down, charges phones in the same place and keeps evenings a bit quieter, it stops being about one person.

Review it together after a couple of weeks. What's working? What isn't? A routine is something you adjust as you go, not a test.

If evenings are busy with homework, our guide to phones and Year 11 homework has some ideas that fit alongside a sleep routine.

When to get more help

Most tiredness in Year 11 improves with a steadier routine. If your teenager stays very tired even after a few weeks of good habits, or you're worried about their sleep, speak to your GP. If they seem low in mood, withdrawn or not themselves, talk to your GP or the school's pastoral team. Schools would much rather know early, and you don't need to have all the answers before you ask.

The short version

  • Agree the routine together, at a calm moment.
  • Keep a steady wake time, including weekends where possible.
  • Build a short, regular wind-down before bed.
  • Phones charge outside the bedroom, by agreement, for the whole household.
  • Keep revision out of bed and drinks caffeine-free later in the evening.
  • Keep the wake time steady through the clocks change and half term.
  • If tiredness persists, speak to your GP. For low mood, your GP or the school's pastoral team.

You can't make a teenager sleep. What you can do is make it easier: a calmer evening, a phone that's out of reach and a morning that starts at the same time. That's a routine worth building together.
