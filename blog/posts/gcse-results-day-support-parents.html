GCSE Results Day Support for Parents: What to Say in the First Five Minutes

Almost every guide to Thursday is about logistics: times, boundaries, next steps. Hardly anyone writes the bit you'll actually need: the words.

The GCSE results day support parents get handed is mostly a list of dates and options. Useful, but it isn't the hard part. The hard part is a specific five minutes on Thursday 20 August 2026, standing in a kitchen or a school hall or on the end of a phone, when an envelope gets opened and your child looks up at you. What you say in that moment tends to be the bit they remember, long after the slip itself has gone in a drawer.

So this isn't a piece about grades. It's a piece about your response, which is the one part of Thursday you actually control.

Why the first five minutes carry so much weight

Your child has spent the last few days in a strange, flat holding pattern. We wrote about that in getting through the wait. By Thursday morning they're running on very little sleep and a lot of adrenaline. In that state, people don't process paragraphs. They process tone, face, and the first sentence.

Which is oddly good news. It means you don't need the perfect speech. You need a warm face and one short, unloaded opener, and then the willingness to stop talking and let them react however they're going to react.

The GCSE results day support parents actually need starts with your face

Whatever you've decided to say, they'll have read your expression first. If you're standing there braced, scanning the paper upside down for the numbers, that's the message, regardless of how gently you phrase what comes next.

The single most useful bit of preparation is deciding, before Thursday, that your first reaction will be to look at them rather than the slip. Let them tell you. Sounds small; it changes the whole shape of the conversation.

Four things worth saying

These work whatever's on the paper, which is rather the point. None of them commits you to a verdict.

"How are you feeling about it?" Not "how did you do?" It hands them the steering wheel and lets them set the tone. It also works when you genuinely can't tell whether they're pleased or crushed, which happens more often than you'd think.

"Take your time. I'm not going anywhere." The most common regret we hear from parents afterwards is rushing. This one line buys everybody five minutes of nothing happening, which on results day is a small gift.

"You worked hard for this. That bit's real either way." It separates the effort from the outcome without pretending the outcome doesn't exist. It's honest, so it lands, and it's true regardless of what the numbers say.

"Do you want to talk about it now, or later?" A genuine question, with "later" as a genuine option. Some teenagers want to unpack it immediately; plenty want to go and see their friends first and think out loud tonight instead. Both are fine.

Four things worth leaving for later

Not because they're wrong, but because Thursday morning is the worst possible slot for them.

Anyone else's results. What their friend got, what their cousin got, what you got in 1994. Comparison in the first five minutes is the fastest way to make a good result feel thin and a hard one feel worse.

"If you'd just…" Even lightly. Even as a joke. There's no version of the retrospective that helps at 8am on results day, and there's a much better version of it in three weeks when everyone's calm.

Instant re-planning. Resits, appeals, alternative colleges, "right, let's ring them now." These routes are all real and all still open next week. We've written up November resits, challenging a grade and what happens if a sixth form says no separately, precisely so nobody has to work them out in the first hour.

Big relief-driven promises. Cars, holidays, phones. If it's a good morning, celebrate, just try to keep it about them rather than about the numbers, which is a distinction worth reading up on in celebrating without piling on pressure.

No big decisions before lunch

If there's one rule to take into Thursday, make it this one. Nothing that gets decided in the first hour has to be decided in the first hour.

Sixth-form and college places, resit entries, appeal requests and course changes all run on timescales measured in days and weeks, not minutes, and the school or college is the right place to sort them, usually with someone on hand that morning or the day after. Adrenaline is a poor advisor. Feed them, let the morning happen, and pick it up in the afternoon with a clearer head.

One practical exception: know in advance how and when your child's results are being handed over, because every school does it slightly differently. Nationally, results are released to students from 8am on Thursday 20 August, but collection times and methods are set by the school, so it's worth a quick check of their email or website tonight rather than at 7:55 on the day. There's more on that in when results come out.

If the day lands heavily

Some results days are quietly hard, and the first five minutes matter even more when they are. The same script holds: warmth first, no verdict, no fixing. If the conversation needs more room than a morning gives it, we've written a longer piece on talking it through when results aren't what they hoped for.

And if your child seems genuinely distressed rather than disappointed (or if you are), it's worth talking to someone outside the house. Samaritans (116 123) is available any time, day or night, for parents as much as for teenagers. Getting in touch early isn't an overreaction.

The one line that covers almost everything

If you remember nothing else on Thursday morning, this does most of the work: "Whatever's on there, we'll sort it. How are you feeling?"

It's unconditional, it's honest, it doesn't pre-judge the paper, and it puts them first. Everything else (the options, the phone calls, the plan) keeps until the afternoon.

For context on where the numbers themselves come from: grades are set by the exam boards and passed on by the school. GCSE Boss is not affiliated with or endorsed by any exam board. We map their published specifications so students can revise exactly what's in their syllabus. If a resit or a fresh start in September ends up on the cards, that's where we can help. If it doesn't, that's fine too. The shape of a good results day is the same either way: look at them, not the slip, and leave the decisions until after lunch.
