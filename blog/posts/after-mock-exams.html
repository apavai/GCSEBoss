After Mock Exams: Writing a Short Debrief Before You Forget

What you do in the hour after mock exams can be more useful than you might think. Right after a paper, you remember things you will have forgotten by the weekend: the question that looked unfamiliar, the moment you ran short of time, the topic you wished you had revised. A short written debrief catches those details while they are fresh, so you have something real to work with when your marked paper comes back.

Mock timing varies from school to school. Some of you have sat them already, some are in the middle of them, and some have them later in the year. This works after any of them, and after any practice paper too.

Why a debrief beats replaying the paper in your head

After a paper, it is natural to go over it again and again, or to compare answers with friends in the corridor. That rarely helps. You end up worrying about one question, or changing your mind about answers you cannot change anyway, and the useful details get lost in the noise.

A debrief is different. It is short, it is written down, and it is about what you noticed rather than how you think you did. It turns a vague feeling ("that went badly" or "that was fine") into a few specific notes you can act on.

What to write after mock exams

Keep it to a few lines per paper. You are not rewriting your answers, and you are not trying to predict a mark. Use three simple prompts.

1. What felt unfamiliar?

Note any question, topic or type of question that caught you off guard. Write down what it was about, even roughly: "the graph question near the end", "the poem I had not looked at closely", "a calculation with units I was not sure about". These are often the most valuable notes, because they point to gaps you can close.

2. Where did time run short?

Did you finish? Was there a question you rushed, or one you spent much longer on than you meant to? Note where it happened. You are not judging yourself here, just recording what happened so you can practise it.

3. What do I want to ask my teacher?

Write down any question you would like to ask when the paper is reviewed in class. It might be how a particular question is marked, what a command word was asking for, or how much to write for a long answer. Having it written down means you will remember to ask.

That is it. Three headings, a few lines each. Five minutes is plenty.

Wait for the marked paper before you judge

This is the most important part. How a paper felt is not a reliable guide to how it went. Some papers feel hard because they are hard for everyone. Some feel easy and turn out to have traps. Your memory of your answers is also less accurate than you think.

So do not try to work out your mark from how you felt walking out. Write your debrief, put it somewhere safe, and leave it until your teacher returns the paper. Then you can compare your notes with what actually happened.

When your marked paper comes back

This is where your debrief earns its place. Sit down with the marked paper, your notes and the mark scheme if your teacher shares it.

  • Check your unfamiliar list. Were those the questions where you dropped marks? Sometimes yes, sometimes you did better than you feared.
  • Check your timing notes. Did the rushed question lose marks? Did a long answer run over and squeeze the questions after it?
  • Ask your questions. Use the review lesson, or catch your teacher afterwards, to ask what you wrote down.

If you have not used a mark scheme closely before, our guide on how to use a mark scheme explains how to read one properly.

Turn your debrief into a short plan

Once you have compared your notes with the marked paper, pick two or three things to work on. Not ten. Two or three specific, manageable things, such as:

  • revisiting one topic that came up as unfamiliar;
  • practising one type of question you found hard to start;
  • doing a short timed section to work on pace.

A GCSE error log is a simple place to keep these, so patterns show up across subjects and papers. And if a topic keeps catching you out, short sessions of active recall are a good way to build it up.

What a debrief is not

A few things are worth leaving out of your notes, because they make the debrief less useful.

  • A predicted mark. Writing "probably a low mark" or "should be fine" adds nothing you can act on, and it may well be wrong.
  • A list of everything you got wrong. You do not know that yet. Stick to what felt unfamiliar or rushed.
  • Other people's answers. What a friend wrote does not tell you how your paper was marked.
  • Harsh comments about yourself. Notes like "I'm useless at this" are not true and do not help. Write what happened, not a verdict.

If you notice your notes sliding into any of these, cross them out and go back to the three prompts. Specific and neutral is the aim.

A simple debrief template

If it helps, copy this into the back of a notebook and fill it in after each paper:

  • Subject and paper:
  • Felt unfamiliar:
  • Time ran short on:
  • Questions for my teacher:
  • After marking, two things to work on:

Leave the last line blank until the paper is back.

Be kind to yourself

Mocks are practice. They exist so you and your teachers can see what to work on before the real thing. Feeling that a paper went badly is common, and it does not tell you much on its own. A short debrief helps you move from that feeling to something useful.

If you are finding mocks stressful, talk to someone at school, such as your form tutor or a teacher you trust. You do not have to work through it on your own.
