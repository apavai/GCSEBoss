What to Do After GCSEs in Summer: Five Things Actually Worth Doing (None of Them Revision)

You did the papers. You walked out of the last one. Now there's a whole summer sitting in front of you with nothing timetabled in it — and honestly, that can feel weirder than it should.

So you're figuring out what to do after GCSEs in summer, and everywhere you look there's someone with an opinion about it. Get a head start on A-levels. Do some “bridging work.” Don't waste the break. Ignore most of that for a second. You've just done the single biggest thing your school year has been building towards for two years. The most useful thing to hear right now is that you've earned the time off, and that using it well doesn't mean cramming more into it. Here are five things genuinely worth doing over the next few weeks — and not one of them is revision.

1. Actually rest — the proper kind

Not “rest” as in scrolling until 3am and waking up feeling worse. Proper rest. For weeks your brain has been running a low background hum of stress: papers to sit, dates to remember, a clock always ticking somewhere. That doesn't switch off the moment the last exam ends. If you feel flat, tired, or a bit lost for the first week or so — that's completely normal, and it's not a sign anything's wrong. It's your nervous system finally being allowed to stand down. Sleep in. Do nothing on purpose. Let the first few days be genuinely lazy without feeling guilty about it. That flat patch lifts on its own once you've caught up on sleep, and everything after it feels easier.

2. See your friends in person

During exams everyone kind of scatters — heads down, different timetables, everyone in their own revision cave. Summer is when you get your people back. Group chats are fine, but they're not the same as actually being somewhere together. Go to the park. Get chips. Have the daft, aimless afternoons where nothing happens and you laugh about nothing for three hours. This is also the last summer before a lot of things change — some friends move to different colleges or sixth forms in September, and the shape of your week shifts. That's not a sad thing, but it does make the ordinary hangouts now worth more than they feel in the moment.

3. Do the thing you kept saying you'd do

Everyone's got one. The instrument you were going to pick back up. The game you wanted to finish. Drawing, coding a silly project, learning to actually cook one good meal, getting back on your bike, writing, filming, whatever it is. During term there was never time, and “I'll do it after exams” became the standard answer. Well — it's after exams. Pick one thing that's just for you, not for a grade or a CV or anyone else, and give it a few afternoons. It's a good reminder that you're a whole person with interests, not just someone who sits exams.

4. Try something new (low stakes, zero pressure)

This is the optional one, and it only counts if it sounds appealing rather than like homework. Summer's a decent window to try something you've never done because there's no downside if it flops. A few ideas that plenty of people your age do over the break: a bit of casual work if you can get it — a few shifts somewhere, helping out at a local thing, cash and a bit of independence are both nice; volunteering for something you actually care about; or learning a small practical skill just because you can. None of this is compulsory and rest absolutely beats forcing it. But if you're the type who gets restless after a fortnight of nothing, having one small new thing on the go can make the summer feel like yours rather than something happening to you.

5. Get outside and move a bit

You spent months sitting at a desk. Your body noticed. You don't need a fitness plan or a gym membership — just get outside and move in a way you don't hate. Walks, swimming, football in the park, a bike ride, dancing round your room, whatever. It sounds almost too simple, but daylight, moving around, and not being hunched over a desk do more for how you feel than pretty much anything else on this list. When the flat, weird post-exam mood is hanging around, a walk outside is one of the most reliable ways to shift it, and it costs nothing. Bonus points if you can rope a friend in — then it's a hangout and a bit of movement at the same time, and you don't have to think of it as “exercise” at all.

What about results and September?

Results day is a fixed date in August, and yes, it'll come round. But it is genuinely weeks away, there is nothing you can do to change the outcome now, and spending July worrying about it just burns a good summer for no reason. When results day gets closer, you can think about it then — and whatever the results are, there are options and people to help you sort out next steps. For now, it's honestly fine to park it. Same with sixth form or college: a little curiosity about what's ahead is healthy, but you do not need to start the course early. The people around you who matter want you to have actually rested when September arrives.

What to do after GCSEs in summer: the short version

If you take one thing from all this: you don't have to earn your summer by being productive. The work is done. So the honest answer to what to do after GCSEs in summer is to rest properly, see the people you like, do one thing that's just for you, try something new only if you fancy it, and get outside. That's it. That's a good summer.

And if you're the kind of person who likes a plan even when you're relaxing, keep it loose: one easy anchor a day is plenty. Something to look forward to in the morning, a bit of company in the afternoon, an early-ish night when you can manage it. You don't need every day mapped out — you've had quite enough timetables for a while. A summer with a bit of shape but no pressure is the sweet spot, and you'll drift into it naturally once the first tired week is behind you. Go and have it — you'll be glad you did.
