GCSE Maths Non-Calculator Paper: What to Practise This Term

The GCSE maths non-calculator paper can feel like the odd one out. You use a calculator in plenty of lessons, then one paper asks you to put it away and do everything by hand. The good news is that the skills it leans on are small, specific and very practisable. A few minutes a day this term goes a long way, and you don't need to wait for mocks to start.

This guide covers what to practise, how to fit it into a normal week and how to check your work without a calculator. It isn't a list of topics that will definitely come up, because nobody can promise that. It's a plan for the number skills that make every other question on the GCSE maths non calculator paper easier.

First, check which paper is which on your course

Different exam boards number their maths papers differently. If you've been searching "GCSE maths paper 1 non calculator", check which paper is which on your course before you plan anything, because the numbering isn't the same everywhere. Your maths teacher, your school's exam information or your course's own website will tell you. In this guide we just call it "the non-calculator paper".

It's also worth knowing that a formulae sheet comes with the paper. That's useful, but it won't do the arithmetic for you. If you can't multiply, divide or work with fractions quickly and accurately by hand, the sheet can't help with that part. Match the sheet to your Foundation or Higher tier, then keep practising the sums.

Mock timing varies by school, too. Some schools hold mocks before Christmas and some after, so ask your teacher when yours are rather than going by what a friend says.

Why GCSE maths non calculator practice matters now

A lot of marks on any maths paper depend on getting the arithmetic right, not just knowing the method. On the non-calculator paper, a small slip in a sum early on can carry through a whole question. That's frustrating when you understood the topic perfectly well.

Autumn term is a good time to sharpen this up, because it doesn't need big revision sessions. It needs regular, short practice. Ten minutes a few times a week, starting now, is easier and more useful than trying to relearn long division the night before a mock.

The five skills to practise first

These are the areas that tend to fall apart once the calculator goes away. Pick one a day and rotate through them.

1. Fractions

Adding, subtracting, multiplying and dividing fractions, plus simplifying and converting between mixed numbers and improper fractions. Fractions turn up inside ratio, probability, algebra and area questions, so getting them fluent pays off everywhere.

A quick routine: write out three fraction sums of each type, do them, then check by working one of them a different way.

2. Percentages

Finding a percentage of an amount, percentage increase and decrease, and writing one number as a percentage of another. Without a calculator, it helps to build from easy pieces. Ten per cent is dividing by ten, five per cent is half of that, one per cent is dividing by a hundred, and you can add those together to reach the percentage you need.

3. Negative numbers

Adding, subtracting, multiplying and dividing with negatives sounds basic, but it's one of the most common places to drop a mark, especially inside algebra. Practise a short set until the sign rules feel automatic, then try substituting negative values into expressions.

4. Written methods

Long multiplication, short and long division, and decimals. Use whichever method your teacher has shown you and stick with it, so it becomes reliable under time pressure. Set it out neatly on squared paper, one digit per box. That alone cuts down on errors.

5. Estimation

Rounding numbers to one significant figure and working out a rough answer. Estimation questions come up in their own right, and it's also your best way to check a long calculation. If your estimate says roughly 600 and your answer says 6,000, something has gone wrong.

A simple weekly plan

You don't need a complicated timetable. Here's one way to fit non-calculator practice into an ordinary school week:

  • Monday: fractions, ten minutes
  • Tuesday: percentages, ten minutes
  • Wednesday: negative numbers, ten minutes
  • Thursday: written methods, ten minutes
  • Friday: estimation and checking, ten minutes
  • Weekend: one short mixed set, or a rest if you've done the week

Put the calculator in a drawer or bag while you practise, not just to one side. Work on paper, by hand, the way you will on the day. If something keeps going wrong, write it down in a short list of errors and come back to that skill the following week.

How to check your answers without a calculator

Checking is part of the skill, not something extra. Try these:

  • Estimate first. Round the numbers and get a rough answer before you start. Compare it with your final answer.
  • Use the inverse. If you divided, multiply back. If you subtracted, add back.
  • Ask if it makes sense. A percentage of an amount should usually be smaller than the amount, unless it's over a hundred per cent. A probability can't be more than one.
  • Reread the question. Make sure you answered what was asked, in the units asked for.

Do your checking in the practice sessions now, so it becomes a habit rather than something you try to remember at the end of a paper.

Non-calculator paper GCSE maths tips for the day itself

When the paper does come round, in a mock or later in the year, a few habits help:

  • Show your working. Even if a final answer is wrong, clear working can still pick up method marks.
  • Lay sums out neatly. Line up place values and leave space. Messy working causes slips.
  • Don't get stuck. If a question isn't working, move on and come back to it.
  • Use the formulae sheet for what it's for. It can remind you of a formula, but you'll still need to do the calculation yourself.
  • Leave a few minutes to check. Go back to the questions with the most arithmetic in them first.

Keep it small and steady

You don't need to love mental arithmetic to do well with it. You just need to practise it often enough that it stops feeling like a separate skill. A few minutes a day this term, calculator off, on the five areas above, will make the non-calculator paper feel much more like the other ones.

If you're not sure which areas to start with, ask your maths teacher which skills they've noticed you need most, or look back at recent classwork for the questions where small slips cost you. Start there, keep it short and keep going.
