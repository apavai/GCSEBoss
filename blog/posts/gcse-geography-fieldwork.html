GCSE Geography Fieldwork: How to Revise Your Own Enquiry So You Can Write About It

GCSE geography fieldwork is one of those things that feels finished the moment you get back on the coach. You collected the data, filled in the clipboard, maybe took a few photos on your phone, and then it quietly slipped out of your head. The catch is that fieldwork isn't coursework. You're asked about it in your written geography exams, so you need to remember what you did, why you did it and what you found. The best time to revise it is now, while it's still fresh.

This guide shows you a simple way to turn each GCSE geography fieldwork enquiry into one clear page you can come back to before mocks and again before the real exams.

How GCSE geography fieldwork is tested

Most GCSE geography courses ask you to carry out at least two enquiries, one physical and one human, somewhere outside your school. You don't hand in a write-up to be marked. Instead, the written exams ask you questions about it.

Those questions tend to come in two flavours. Some are about your own enquiries: what question you were investigating, how you collected your data, how you presented it and what you'd do differently. Others give you fieldwork you've never seen before, such as a table, a map or a set of results from somebody else's investigation, and ask you to interpret it or comment on how it was done.

Exactly how this works depends on your course, so ask your geography teacher which exam includes fieldwork and what kind of questions to expect. They'll know your specification inside out.

Why your own enquiry is so easy to forget

You might have done your fieldwork months ago, in a single day, with a lot going on. You were thinking about the weather, your group and getting the readings done, not about how you'd explain your sampling method in an exam.

That means the details fade fast. Where exactly were you? Why that place? How many readings did you take, and how did you choose where to take them? What did the graph actually show? If you can't answer those clearly now, that's completely normal. It just means it's time to write it down properly.

Build one page per enquiry

The simplest fix is one revision page for each enquiry. Keep it to a single side of paper, or one note on your phone, so it stays easy to review. Use the same headings every time.

  1. Your enquiry question. Write the question or hypothesis you were testing in one sentence. If you can't remember the exact wording, check your class notes or ask your teacher.
  2. Where and why there. Name the place you went and give a reason it suited your question. Think about access, safety and whether the place actually let you test your idea.
  3. How you collected your data. List the methods you used, in order. Note whether the data was primary (collected by you) or secondary (from somewhere else), and how you chose where or when to sample.
  4. One table or graph. Sketch the main way you presented your results. Label the axes. You don't need every number, just enough to describe the pattern.
  5. What it showed. Write two or three sentences on what you found and whether it answered your question.
  6. One limitation. Pick one thing that made your results less reliable. Maybe the weather changed, your sample was small, or different people measured things in slightly different ways.
  7. One improvement. Say how you'd do it better next time, and why that change would help.

That's it. Seven headings, one page. Do it for your physical enquiry and your human enquiry, and you've got the core of your fieldwork revision done.

Practise explaining your choices out loud

Fieldwork questions often want you to justify a decision, not just describe it. Why did you use that sampling method? Why was that location suitable? Why did you present the data that way?

A quick way to practise is to cover your page and explain each heading out loud in plain sentences, as if you were telling a friend who wasn't there. If you get stuck, look back, then try again tomorrow. Explaining it in your own words is the part that sticks.

Try starting your answers with phrases like "We chose this because..." or "This was a limitation because...". The word "because" pushes you to give a reason, which is usually what the question is after.

GCSE geography fieldwork questions on data you've never seen

The unfamiliar questions can feel harder, but they test the same skills. When you see somebody else's results, ask yourself the same things you'd ask about your own:

  • What were they trying to find out?
  • How did they collect the data, and is that method fair?
  • What pattern does the table or graph show?
  • Is there anything odd, like a result that doesn't fit?
  • What would make the investigation more reliable?

Your own fieldwork page is the perfect warm-up for this. Once you can pick apart your own enquiry, picking apart somebody else's feels much more familiar. Practice questions from your teacher or past papers your school shares are great for this.

If your data has gaps

Lots of students find their notes are patchy. A missing reading, a smudged table, a photo that didn't save. Please don't go back to the site on your own to redo anything. Instead, talk to your geography teacher. They may have the class data, a shared spreadsheet or photos from the day.

Gaps can actually be useful. If something went wrong with your data collection, that's a ready-made limitation you can write about, along with how you'd avoid it next time.

Fit it in around the rest of your revision

You don't need a big block of time for this. One enquiry page is a solid half-hour job. Do the physical one this week and the human one next week, then add them to the topics you test yourself on.

If you keep an error log, add any fieldwork questions you get wrong in class. Our guide to keeping a GCSE error log explains how. And because fieldwork often links to the places you've studied, it's worth revising it alongside your case studies. Our post on GCSE geography case studies has a simple method you can use for those too.

A quick fieldwork checklist

Before mocks, check you can answer these for each enquiry without looking:

  • What question were you investigating?
  • Where did you go, and why was it a good place for that question?
  • Which methods did you use, and how did you choose your sample?
  • How did you present your data?
  • What did you find?
  • What was one limitation, and how would you improve it?

If you can talk through all six for both enquiries, you're in a strong place for whatever fieldwork questions come up.

Keep it small and keep it going

GCSE geography fieldwork revision isn't about writing an essay. It's about having one clear page per enquiry and being able to explain it in your own words. Do it now, while you still remember the day, and future you will be very glad you did.
