Talking to Your Teenager After a GCSE Exam: When They Won't Say a Word

They get in the car, or they come through the door, and you ask the only question on your mind — "how did it go?" — and you get a shrug, a "fine", or nothing at all. If you're finding talking to your teenager after a GCSE exam almost impossible right now, you are not doing anything wrong, and neither are they. The silence is one of the most normal things in the world.

We're in the thick of the exam window — papers are being sat for real this fortnight, with the last ones on 26 June — so this scene is playing out in kitchens and on school runs all over the country this week. A teenager walks out of a paper they've spent months building towards, and the very last thing they want to do is relive it out loud for a worried parent. The instinct to ask is completely understandable. But the quiet you get back is rarely the bad sign it feels like. Here's what's usually going on, and the small, low-pressure things that help far more than a debrief.

Why post-exam silence is so normal

An exam is two or three hours of sustained, high-stakes concentration. By the time your teenager puts their pen down, their brain has been running at full tilt and is completely spent. What they need next isn't analysis — it's decompression. Talking through every question means going back into the room they've just fought their way out of, and most teenagers simply don't have it in them in that first hour. On top of that, they often genuinely don't know yet how it went. Exams are strange like that: the paper that felt like a disaster can mark up fine, and the one that felt easy can be the one where everyone misread the same question. Their silence isn't a verdict. It's a tired person who hasn't processed it yet, and would quite like a snack.

What's actually going on in their head

There's also a self-protective bit worth understanding. If a paper went badly, the last thing a 15- or 16-year-old wants is to say so out loud and watch your face change. Saying nothing keeps the possibility open that it was fine — and protects them from feeling they've let you down. So the shrug is often less "leave me alone" and more "I can't handle a big conversation about this yet, please don't make me." When you read it that way, the pressure comes off both of you. You're not being shut out. You're being asked, in the only language they've got right now, for a bit of room.

Talking to your teenager after a GCSE exam: keep it low-key

The single most useful thing you can probably do is make the homecoming about anything other than the exam. "Want a snack?" beats "how did it go?" almost every time. A drink, something to eat, the dog, what's for tea, a completely unrelated bit of family news — anything that signals normal life is still here and they don't have to perform a post-mortem to be welcome in it. If they want to talk, they will, and it'll usually come sideways and later: in the car a day on, over the washing up, at 10pm through a bedroom door. Your job in that first hour isn't to extract information. It's to be the calm, unbothered presence that makes it safe to talk when they're ready — which is almost always once they've eaten and switched off for a bit.

What to avoid

A few things reliably make the silence worse. The play-by-play interrogation — "what came up? what did you put for question three? did you finish?" — turns a tired teenager into a defendant and teaches them that coming home means an exam about the exam. Comparing notes with friends or siblings ("your sister said that paper was easy") lands as a judgement even when it isn't meant as one. And catastrophising on their behalf — "oh no, you look upset, was it awful?" — hands them your anxiety to carry on top of their own. None of this is about walking on eggshells. It's just that the questions that feel most natural to ask are often the ones that close the door, and a lighter touch keeps it open.

When a paper genuinely didn't go to plan

Sometimes a paper really does go wrong — a question misread, time misjudged, or simply an off day, which everyone is entitled to. Exam papers don't always go to plan, and even the boards occasionally get things wrong at their end. If your teenager comes home unusually flat after a specific paper, the most reassuring thing you can offer is perspective, gently and without naming a number: one paper is one paper, results are built from a whole suite of them, and a quiet evening isn't a verdict on any of it. You don't need to fix it or talk them out of how they feel — just to be steady. "That sounds like a rough one. It's done now. Let's get you fed" does more than any pep talk about effort or outcomes.

When quiet is worth a gentle check-in

Most post-exam silence is ordinary tiredness and clears within an evening. It's worth paying quiet attention, though, if the flatness doesn't lift — if they're not eating, not sleeping, withdrawing from everything rather than just decompressing, or carrying a level of distress that feels bigger than one paper. That's no longer about exam etiquette; that's your teenager telling you they're struggling, and it deserves a low-key, no-pressure check-in: "you've seemed really wiped out this week — anything you want to talk about, or shall I just leave you to it?" Most of the time the answer is "I'm fine, just tired", and that's genuinely that. But you know your own child, and trusting that instinct matters more than any rule of thumb.
