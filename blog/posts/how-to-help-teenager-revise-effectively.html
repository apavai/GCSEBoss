How to Help Your Teenager Revise Effectively (Why One Subject All Day Doesn't Work)

Somewhere around mid-morning, your teenager announces the plan: "I'm just going to do Maths all day." It sounds like commitment. It is, unfortunately, close to the least efficient revision plan available to them.

If you want to know how to help your teenager revise effectively, the most useful thing you can probably do is gently question the all-day single-subject session. Not because it's lazy — it's the opposite, it's usually the most conscientious child in the house who proposes it — but because of what actually happens to a brain somewhere around hour three of the same topic.

This isn't a lecture about willpower or screen time. It's about two fairly boring ideas that happen to be among the best-evidenced things we know about learning: spacing (spreading sessions out over time) and interleaving (switching between subjects or topics rather than blocking them).

Why the all-day Maths session feels so productive

Here's the trap. When your teenager grinds through six hours of quadratic equations, something genuinely improves: their performance during that session. By question forty they're faster and more fluent than they were at question five. That feels exactly like learning.

The problem is that fluency is mostly temporary. They're not retrieving the method fresh each time — it's still loaded, sitting in working memory from the last question. Nothing is forcing them to reconstruct it. And reconstruction is the bit that builds durable memory.

So they finish the day feeling capable, having built relatively little that survives to August. Then in three weeks they sit a past paper, meet the same topic cold among fifteen others, and can't find it. The session felt good and taught less than it promised.

What interleaving actually means

Interleaving just means mixing. Instead of Maths, Maths, Maths, they do Maths, then Chemistry, then Maths again, then a bit of French vocabulary. Instead of thirty questions on one type of equation, they do a mixed set where each question requires them to first work out what kind of problem this is.

That extra step — identifying which method applies before applying it — is the whole point. In a blocked session it's free, because every question is the same kind. In an exam it's the hardest part, because nothing tells them which topic a question belongs to. Interleaving practises the skill the exam actually tests.

It also feels worse. Mixed practice is slower, more error-prone in the moment, and less satisfying than a clean run of thirty correct answers. Studies of distributed and mixed practice consistently find that learners rate these methods as less effective than blocking, while remembering more from them later.

Source: Dunlosky et al., "Improving Students' Learning With Effective Learning Techniques," Psychological Science in the Public Interest, 2013.

Spacing: the same hours, spread out

Spacing is the sibling idea, and it's about the calendar rather than the session. Six hours of Maths on Saturday and six hours of Chemistry on Sunday is two blocked days. Two hours of each, across three days, is the same twelve hours arranged so that each subject gets revisited after a gap.

The gap is doing the work. When your teenager comes back to a topic after a day or two, they've partly forgotten it, and the effortful business of dragging it back is what makes it stickier next time. Comfortable, immediate recall builds very little. Slightly difficult recall builds a lot.

This is genuinely good news for a household, because it means the answer isn't "more hours." The same hours, rearranged, do more. There's a fuller walkthrough of the intervals in our post on a spaced repetition schedule for GCSE, and the retrieval side of it in active recall.

How to help your teenager revise effectively without hovering

The useful intervention here is small. You're not redesigning their timetable, and you almost certainly shouldn't try — a revision plan imposed by a parent tends to have a short and resentful life.

What tends to land better is a question rather than an instruction. "How many subjects are you touching today?" is a more productive thing to ask than "you should switch subjects." If the answer is one, the follow-up more or less asks itself.

A few things that are worth knowing, if it comes up:

Blocks of roughly forty-five minutes to an hour per subject are plenty. Three different subjects in a day is a reasonable target, and two is fine. Switching should happen at a natural stopping point, not mid-problem — interleaving is about mixing topics across a session, not abandoning things half-finished. And a subject touched briefly on three days beats the same subject hammered once.

The other thing worth saying out loud: if a mixed session feels harder and messier than a blocked one, that's the method working, not failing. Teenagers are quite reasonably inclined to abandon a technique that makes them feel less competent. Knowing in advance that the difficulty is the point makes it considerably easier to stick with.

What this looks like on a normal Tuesday

Concretely: an hour of Maths after school, a short break, then forty-five minutes of Biology, then twenty minutes going back over the Maths topics from Sunday. Three subjects, one of them revisited across a gap. That's spacing and interleaving in a single evening, and it took no more time than an evening of Maths alone.

Tomorrow, Maths doesn't appear at all. It comes back on Thursday, by which point it's been partly forgotten, which is precisely what you want.

None of this requires a spreadsheet, though some teenagers love one. It mostly requires that the day has more than one subject in it.
