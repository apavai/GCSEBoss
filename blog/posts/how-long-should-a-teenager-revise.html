How Long Should a Teenager Revise in One Sitting?

It's the final exam week, you glance at the clock, and the maths is doing itself in your head: they were only at the desk for half an hour. So how long should a teenager revise in one sitting, and is short actually a problem? Usually not. Here's the calmer way to think about it.

Somewhere in the last week of GCSEs, most parents have the same quiet worry. Your teenager disappears upstairs to revise, comes back down what feels like five minutes later, and you're left wondering whether that counted as anything at all. The instinct is to push for more: a longer session, a proper afternoon at the desk, the kind of marathon that looks like hard work. Before you do, it's worth knowing what the research and the teachers we talk to actually say about revision session length. The short version: when parents ask how long should a teenager revise in one go, shorter is often better, and the hours you're counting aren't the thing that matters most. What follows is the calmer framework, useful in the final week, and just as useful in the quieter months before it.

So how long should a teenager revise in one sitting?

For most teenagers, a focused block of around 25 to 40 minutes, followed by a genuine break, is the sweet spot. It's long enough to get properly into a topic and short enough that their attention doesn't quietly drift off somewhere around the twenty-minute mark. There's nothing magic about a specific number (some teenagers do better in 25-minute bursts, others can hold a clean 45), but once you push much past an hour without a break, you're usually paying for time with quality. The honest answer to "how long should a teenager revise" is therefore less about the length of one session and more about how focused that session is, and how often it happens.

Why focus fades faster than you'd think

Attention isn't a tap you leave running. It's more like a muscle that tires, and for a tired, mid-exam-season teenager, it tires quickly. After the first stretch of real concentration, the brain starts cutting corners: re-reading the same paragraph, copying notes out without taking anything in, reaching for the phone "just to check something." None of that is laziness. It's the completely normal shape of human focus. A three-hour session doesn't give you three hours of revision; it gives you maybe forty good minutes and a long, demoralising tail of pretending. Two focused blocks with a break in between will almost always beat that single long slog.

The case for the short, focused block

There's a reason teachers keep nudging students towards shorter sessions. When the finish line is close (say, half an hour away), it's far easier to stay properly switched on, because the brain can see the end. That sense of "I only have to concentrate until the break" is exactly what keeps the revision active rather than passive. And active revision is the part that does the work: testing yourself, covering the page and trying to recall it, attempting a question before checking the answer. You can't really do that for three hours straight. You can do it brilliantly for thirty minutes. "But they only did half an hour"

Here's the worry to put down. If your teenager did a focused half-hour, came up for air, and then did another focused half-hour later in the evening, that is not a thin day of revision. That's a good one. The "not enough hours" anxiety is one of the most common things parents feel in the final week, and it's almost always measuring the wrong thing. Nobody gets marked on time served at the desk. They get marked on what they can do in the exam hall, and what they can do is built far more reliably by regular, focused practice than by the occasional heroic Sunday. A short session that actually happened beats a long one that turned into two hours of staring out the window.

Helping without hovering

The most useful thing you can probably do is protect the conditions, not police the clock. That means a quiet-ish corner, the phone parked in another room during the focused block, and a real break afterwards that isn't just scrolling: a walk to the kitchen, a drink, ten minutes of something that isn't a screen. You don't need to sit beside them with a stopwatch. You especially don't need to ask "have you done enough?" every evening, which tends to land as pressure rather than support. A simple shape for the evening

If it helps to picture it, here's a shape that works for a lot of families without anyone having to enforce it. After dinner, one focused block of about half an hour on a single topic: not "do some revision," but something specific, like "test myself on the required practicals" or "do one past-paper question and mark it." Then a real break: off the chair, away from the desk, no screen if you can manage it. Later, if there's energy left, a second short block on something different. Two clean half-hours like that, most evenings, add up to a genuinely solid week, and they leave enough of the evening intact that your teenager doesn't start to dread the whole thing. The aim is a rhythm they could actually keep up, not a one-off blowout that leaves them flat the next day. If they're not sure which topics to point those blocks at, the honest move is to start with the ones that feel uncomfortable rather than the ones that feel familiar. That's where the marks are usually hiding.

What this looks like in the final exam week

In the closing days, the temptation to cram harder gets stronger, and it's worth resisting on your teenager's behalf. This late, the goal isn't to pack in more hours. It's to keep the good habits steady and the sleep intact. A couple of focused blocks a day, aimed at the topics that genuinely still need work rather than the comfortable ones, will do more than a panicked all-dayer that wrecks tonight's sleep and tomorrow's exam. If you only take one thing from this, let it be the calm one: a focused half-hour is not too little. It's often exactly right, and the fact that they got up and did it at all, this deep into exam season, is the bit worth noticing.
