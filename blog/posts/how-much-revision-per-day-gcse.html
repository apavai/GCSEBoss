How Much Revision a Day for GCSE? The Honest Answer

Not the number you've seen on a colour-coded timetable someone posted at midnight. The number you can actually hit on a Tuesday when you're tired.

If you've searched how much revision a day for GCSE, you've probably already found the answers that make you feel slightly ill. Six hours. Eight hours. A grid with every waking hour blocked out in a different colour. Someone in the comments saying they did ten and it was fine.

Here's the honest version, and it's shorter than you're expecting. Around two focused hours a day, most days, spread across a few subjects. In the last few weeks before exams, more — but even then, less than the internet claims. That's it. That's the number. What follows is why it works, and why the six-hour version mostly doesn't.

Why two hours beats six

Not because two hours is enough work. Because six hours isn't really six hours.

When people sit down for a six-hour session, what actually happens is roughly ninety minutes of real concentration, then a long tail of re-reading the same page, checking your phone, highlighting things you already know, and staring at a paragraph about enzymes until the words stop meaning anything. You feel exhausted at the end of it, because you were sitting at a desk for six hours. But the exhaustion isn't proof of learning. It's just proof of sitting.

Two hours where you're actually thinking — testing yourself, getting things wrong, fixing them — will move more into long-term memory than six hours of low-effort contact with your notes. The tiring bit is the bit that works. If revision feels completely comfortable, it's probably not doing much.

Spread the same hours out and you get more from them

There's a well-established finding in the memory research: the same total study time produces stronger, longer-lasting recall when it's spread across several days rather than packed into one. It's called distributed practice, and in a major review of learning techniques it came out as one of the few strategies with genuinely strong evidence behind it.

Source: Dunlosky et al., Improving Students' Learning With Effective Learning Techniques, Psychological Science in the Public Interest, 2013.

Practically: four separate thirty-minute sessions across four days will do more for you than one two-hour block on Sunday. Same two hours. Different result. The gap between sessions is not wasted time — the small amount of forgetting that happens in the gap is exactly what makes the next session stick.

What two hours actually looks like

Vague plans die on contact with a Tuesday. Concrete ones survive. So:

Four blocks of about thirty minutes. Two or three different subjects, not one. A short break between each — properly away from the desk, not scrolling in the same chair. In each block, one job: not "do Chemistry", but "close the book and write down everything I can remember about rates of reaction, then check what I missed."

That last bit is the whole game. Writing from memory feels worse than re-reading, because you're confronted with the gaps. Re-reading feels great and teaches you almost nothing. The uncomfortable one is the useful one.

Some days you'll do twenty minutes. That's fine.

There's a version of revision advice that treats every missed session as a moral failure, and it's rubbish. You had double PE, you got home late, your head hurts. Twenty minutes of proper retrieval on a bad day is worth more than nothing, and considerably more than the three hours you're planning to feel guilty about instead.

The thing that actually predicts a calm exam season isn't the size of your best day. It's how few zero days you have. Consistency is boring advice because it's true. A short session you actually do beats a long session you keep rescheduling.

How much revision a day for GCSE as exams get closer?

Yes, sensibly. In the autumn and through mock season, two hours on school days is a solid, sustainable baseline — enough to keep everything ticking over without eating your whole life. In the final six to eight weeks, most people ramp up: three hours on a school day, more at weekends, and past-paper work starts taking over from topic revision.

But the ramp only works if you weren't already flat out. That's the real argument for the unglamorous two hours: it leaves you somewhere to go. Start in October doing six-hour days and by March you're exhausted, resentful, and have nothing left to increase. Steady beats heroic, every time.

How to stop the number becoming the point

Hours are the easiest thing to measure and the least useful thing to track. Nobody walks into an exam and is asked how long they sat at a desk. A far better question at the end of a session: what can I now do that I couldn't do an hour ago?

If the answer is "I can explain osmosis without looking", that was a good session, whatever the clock says. If the answer is "I have a very neat page of notes about osmosis", you've done handwriting practice.

Thirty minutes is the default session length for a reason: it's about as long as most people can think hard before the returns start dropping.

The short version

Two focused hours a day, most days. Four thirty-minute blocks, a few different subjects, testing yourself rather than reading at yourself. More in the final stretch. Some days less, and no drama about it. The aim is to arrive at study leave with your subjects broadly covered and yourself still functioning — not to win an argument with a stranger's timetable.

You've probably been doing more than you think, and worrying about it more than it deserves. Two hours. Go and do something else with the rest of the evening.
