How to Talk to Your Teenager About Bad GCSE Results: What to Say, What to Skip

If you're bracing for a results day that might not go the way your teenager hoped, the most useful thing you can prepare isn't a plan. It's the conversation. Here's what to say, what to skip, and why the first hour matters far less than it feels like it will.

GCSE results land on Thursday 20 August 2026. For most families the day passes quietly and happily. But some teenagers will open that slip and find grades that fall short of what they were hoping for, and if that's the moment you're quietly dreading, it helps to think ahead about it now. Knowing how to talk to your teenager about bad GCSE results isn't about having the perfect speech ready. It's about knowing which few things genuinely help in the first hour, and which well-meant instincts are better left unsaid.

The parents and teachers we talk to tend to agree on one thing: on a hard results day, your teen isn't looking for a solution from you. They're looking for a signal that the ground is still solid. Almost everything below follows from that.

Prepare the conversation before the envelope

You don't need a script, but it helps to have decided, in advance, what your opening is going to be, because the opening sets the tone for everything after. Pick something short, warm and outcome-neutral that you can say whatever the grades turn out to be: "Whatever's on that slip, we'll sort it out together." Say a version of it the night before, and again before they look. It quietly tells your teen that your reaction isn't riding on a number, which is exactly the reassurance that lets them show you a disappointing result instead of hiding it.

It's also worth deciding in advance where you'll be. A teenager checking results with a parent hovering over their shoulder feels very different from one who knows you're in the next room and happy to be called in. Offer the choice and follow their lead.

What to say in the first five minutes

Lead with feeling, not fixing. "That's really disappointing. I can see it's not what you wanted" lands far better than any immediate talk of options. Name the emotion, sit with it for a moment, and resist the urge to leap to a plan. A few phrases that tend to help:

"I'm proud of the work you put in. That hasn't changed." · "You don't have to decide anything today." · "Take as long as you need, then we'll look at it together when you're ready." Each one does the same quiet job: it separates your teen's worth from their grades, and it takes the pressure off the clock. Results day feels enormous and permanent in the moment, and it is genuinely neither, but that's something they'll believe more easily if they hear it as calm company rather than a pep talk.

What to skip

Some of the least helpful things parents say come from love, which is what makes them easy to slip into. Try to skip the following, at least on the day itself. Avoid "I told you that you should have revised more." Even if it's true, it's not useful now and it teaches your teen not to come to you. Skip the instant comparison to a sibling, a cousin or a friend's child; it lands as a verdict, not encouragement. Steer clear of catastrophising ("this ruins your chances of…"), because it almost never turns out to be accurate and it makes a heavy moment heavier. And go easy on relentless positivity: "it doesn't matter at all!" can feel dismissive when to them, right now, it clearly does matter. You don't have to pretend the result is fine. You just have to make clear that they are.

How to talk to your teenager about bad GCSE results without making it about you

Disappointing grades can stir up a parent's own feelings: worry, frustration, even a flicker of embarrassment about what to tell relatives. Those feelings are normal, but the results conversation isn't the place to process them. If you need to vent or think aloud, do it with another adult, later. In the room with your teen, keep the focus on them. A good private check before you speak is simple: "Is this for them, or is this for me?" If it's for you, save it. Their job on results day is to feel supported enough to face the next step; your job is to be the steady one.

Move to next steps: just not today

There are real, practical options after a disappointing set of results, and it's genuinely reassuring for a teenager to know they exist, but they almost never need to be actioned in the first hour. If a grade is close to what's needed, schools and colleges can ask the exam board for a review of marking, and each board has a deadline (the request comes through the school, not from you directly). If a grade needs improving, resits are available: English and maths in the November 2026 series, most other subjects the following summer. And the single most useful conversation is often the one with the sixth form or college your teen is heading to: entry requirements have more give in them than the printed prospectus suggests, especially once a place is nearly met. Mention that these paths exist, then park the detail for a day or two. Big decisions made in the first rush of disappointment are rarely the best ones.

Where to get support if it's hitting hard

For most teenagers a disappointing results day is a bump, not a wall: a few flat days, a plan that shifts a little, then normal life. But if it's hitting your teen harder than that (if they can't sleep, seem hopeless, or are pulling away from everyone), take it seriously and don't wait it out alone. Talk to your GP, their school or college, or a confidential service: Childline (0800 1111) is free and open 24/7 for anyone under 19, and Samaritans (116 123) is there any time, day or night, for you as well as for them. Reaching out early isn't an overreaction. It's just a sensible thing to do when a day lands heavily.
