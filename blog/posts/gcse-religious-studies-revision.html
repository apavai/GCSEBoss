GCSE Religious Studies Revision: How to Keep Two Contrasting Viewpoints Distinct

The part of GCSE religious studies revision that quietly costs people marks isn't remembering the content. It's keeping two contrasting views separate once you're under exam pressure. You revise a religious viewpoint and a non-religious one, you walk in confident, and somewhere around the third question they melt into one beige answer that sort of agrees with everyone. Here's a simple scaffold that keeps them apart on the page.

First, the reassuring bit: if you're reading this near the end of the exam window, you've basically done the hard part already. The content is in there. What's left is a structure problem, not a knowledge problem, and structure problems are the easiest kind to fix, because you can fix them with a habit rather than another three hours of cramming.

Why two viewpoints blur together

RS questions love asking you to weigh up two positions: a religious view and a non-religious view, or two different religious traditions disagreeing with each other. The trap is that you've revised them as a big pile of "things people believe about this topic," so when you write, you reach into the pile and pull out whatever surfaces first. The result reads like one person having a slightly confused argument with themselves.

The examiner isn't testing whether you personally agree with anyone. They're testing whether you can hold two distinct positions, explain why each side holds it, and show how they actually differ. If your two viewpoints sound like the same idea wearing two hats, that distinction collapses, and the distinction is the whole point of the question.

The scaffold: belief, reason, contrast, evaluation

Here's the repeatable shape. For any contrasting-views question, run each position through four steps in the same order every time:

Belief: state the position plainly. "One religious teaching is that…" or "A Humanist view is that…". Reason: why is that position held? Source of authority, a key teaching, a principle. Contrast: say explicitly how the second position differs from the first, not just what it is. Evaluation: a brief, fair line on which argument is stronger and why. Belief, reason, contrast, evaluation. Same order, every single time, until your hand does it without you thinking.

The magic is in doing it in that fixed order. When the structure is automatic, you're not deciding what to write next under pressure. You're just filling in four boxes you already know. That frees up all your brain for the actual content.

Keep the two columns physically separate

When you revise a contrasting-views topic, draw a line down the middle of the page. Position A on the left, Position B on the right. Same four rows on each side (belief, reason, contrast, evaluation), filled in for both. You'll see instantly if you've got a gap, like a belief with no reason behind it, or a "contrast" row that just repeats the belief.

Two columns isn't just a revision aid. It's a mental model you carry into the exam. Even when you're writing in continuous prose, you can picture which column you're in and finish that thought before you cross over. The students who blur their viewpoints are almost always the ones revising both positions jumbled together in one block of notes.

Trap one: naming a view without its reason

"Some people think it's wrong. Other people think it's fine." That's two beliefs and zero reasons, and it's worth almost nothing because anyone could write it without revising at all. The reason is where the marks live: the teaching, the principle, the source of authority that makes a position more than a personal opinion. If you can't say why a group holds a view, you haven't actually revised that view yet; you've just memorised a label.

Trap two: collapsing "contrast" into "the opposite"

"Contrast" doesn't mean "and the other lot think the opposite." Real contrast names the specific point of difference: they might agree on the goal but disagree on the method, or share a value but rank it differently against another value. Two positions can both care deeply about, say, the sanctity of life and still land in different places because they weigh it against different things. Spotting where the disagreement actually sits is the bit that reads as understanding rather than memorising.

Handle every belief with respect

One thing worth saying plainly: your job in RS is to explain positions accurately and fairly, not to judge them. That goes for religious and non-religious views alike. Write about each one the way someone who holds it would recognise as fair, even (especially) the ones you disagree with. It's the right thing to do, and it also happens to be exactly what a high-level evaluation looks like: balanced, accurate, and not secretly arguing for one side the whole way through.

GCSE religious studies revision that sticks

Like any technique, the scaffold only works if you drill it on real questions, so don't just nod at this and move on. Take past-paper contrasting-views questions for your board and run every one through the four steps with an actual two-column grid, even the ones that look easy. The point is to make the shape automatic, so that on the day your hand reaches for it before your brain has time to panic. It's also worth knowing that the exact contrasting viewpoints you need vary by exam board and by the religions you're studying, so "RS" isn't one fixed syllabus. The one-line version

Two columns, four rows each (belief, reason, contrast, evaluation), in the same order every time. Name the reason, not just the view. Make the contrast a real point of difference, not a mirror image. Treat every position fairly. Do that and the question that used to turn into one blurry answer becomes two clean, confident ones. You've done the work. Now go and keep them apart.
