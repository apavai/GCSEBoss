GCSE Vocabulary Revision for French, Spanish and German: Little and Often

GCSE vocabulary revision feels endless when you try to learn a huge list in one sitting. You stare at a page, copy it out, and by the next day half of it has gone. The fix is not a longer session. It is a smaller set, done often, with your own voice and a quick test both ways.

Use the list your teacher gave you. Your school knows what is on your course.

Why little and often beats cramming

Your brain remembers words better when you meet them several times, with gaps in between, than when you hammer one long list once. That is the same idea behind active recall: you pull the answer out, rather than rereading it.

Ten focused minutes before tea, on the bus or after school beat a two-hour Sunday cram. As the evenings draw in, short sets are easier to protect.

GCSE vocabulary revision in five habits

Keep these five habits. They matter more than fancy stationery.

1. Work from your school list

Ask your teacher which list or booklet you should be using. Different schools and courses organise vocabulary differently. Do not invent a mega-list from random websites.

If your list is huge, break it into topics or weeks the way your teacher already has. Your job is to learn the next small chunk well, not to finish every word tonight.

2. Take a small set each day

Pick a set you can finish in about ten minutes. That might be a short page, a topic box or a handful of cards. Write the English on one side and the target language on the other, or use whatever format your teacher prefers.

When the set feels easy, add a few older words you have already met. Mixing new and old is how words stay with you.

3. Say every word out loud

Silent reading is weak practice for languages. Say the French, Spanish or German word clearly, then the English, then the other way round. If you feel silly, do it quietly at home or mouth it on the bus. Hearing yourself say it helps you spot sounds you would skip on the page.

This also feeds your speaking exam. Short, clear pronunciation now makes speaking practice less scary later.

4. Test both directions

Cover one side and recall the other. Then swap. Knowing "dog" means the foreign word is not the same as recognising the foreign word and finding "dog". Both directions matter in listening, reading, writing and speaking.

Mark the ones you miss and put them at the front of tomorrow's set. Do not rewrite the whole list every day. Just move the sticky ones forward.

5. Put each word in a short sentence

Once you can recall a word both ways, use it. Make one short sentence aloud or on paper. It does not need to be clever. "I like apples" or "The train is late" is enough. Using the word is how it stops being a flashcard and starts being something you can actually say.

How to learn GCSE French vocab without drowning

French lists often look long because of gender and plurals. Keep the article with the noun from day one (le, la, un, une) so you are not learning the word twice. Group words by topic the way your booklet does: school, home, free time, and so on.

When a word will not stick, check whether you are saying it wrong. Ask a teacher or a classmate who is confident, then say it correctly three times. Wrong pronunciation is hard to unlearn later.

If you have a speaking or writing task coming up, pull only the words you need for that topic into a mini-set for a few days. That is still little and often. It is just aimed at the next piece of work.

GCSE Spanish vocabulary revision that fits a busy week

Spanish spelling is more regular than English, which helps once you know the sounds. Say new words slowly the first time, then at normal speed. Watch for false friends and lookalikes that mean something else. When two words look similar, put them on the same card with a clear note so you do not mix them up.

A week of Spanish vocab can look like this:

  • Monday: new set, say aloud both ways
  • Tuesday: same set, plus five from last week
  • Wednesday: short sentences with the sticky words
  • Thursday: mix old topics for five minutes
  • Friday: quick check, then a short break for the weekend

Adjust the days if you have clubs or a late night. The pattern matters more than the exact timetable.

German: compounds and genders without the stress

German compounds look scary until you split them. Cover the second half, say the first half, then put them back together. Keep the article with every noun. Gender mistakes are common, and fixing them early is easier than guessing in the exam hall.

If your list includes separable verbs, learn the main form and one simple example sentence. You do not need a grammar lecture every night. You need the word to come out when you need it.

A half-term friendly plan

Half term dates vary by school, so check your own calendar. When you have a break, keep a tiny daily habit rather than stopping completely. Five minutes of old cards is enough to stop the "I knew this last month" feeling when term starts again.

Use daylight if you can. A short set on a bench, in the kitchen or before you put your bag down beats waiting until you are tired and scrolling. Phones parked elsewhere for those ten minutes helps more than another colour of highlighter.

What not to do

  • Do not promise yourself you will "do all the vocab this weekend". You will not, and then you will feel behind.
  • Do not copy lists without testing yourself. Copying feels productive. Testing is what works.
  • Do not name or rely on random revision apps as your only plan. Your school list and your own voice are the core.
  • Do not skip words you find boring. Those are often the ones that turn up in listening and reading.

How to know it is working

You will notice smaller wins first. A word you used to blank on comes out in a sentence. A listening clip feels less like noise. A writing task needs less dictionary time. That is enough. You do not need a dramatic overnight change.

If a whole topic still feels blank after a week of honest practice, ask your teacher which part to prioritise. They would rather help early than hear about it the night before a test.
