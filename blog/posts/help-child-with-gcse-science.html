Help Your Child With GCSE Science When It Is Not Your Subject

Many parents want to help their child with GCSE science and feel stuck the moment they see a page of equations, cell diagrams or chemical symbols. If science was never your favourite subject at school, or you have not looked at it for twenty years, it is easy to assume you cannot be much use. You can. Helping a teenager revise science does not mean re-learning biology, chemistry and physics yourself. It means giving them a calm audience to explain ideas to, helping them check against their own notes, and keeping revision organised.

Mock timing varies from school to school. Some Year 11 students have already sat science mocks, some are sitting them now, and some schools hold them later in the year. The habits below work at any point, whether a paper is next week or several months away.

Check whether they study Combined or Triple Science

Before you look at any revision material, find out which science route your child is taking. Most schools enter students for either Combined Science, which covers biology, chemistry and physics together, or Separate Sciences, often called Triple Science, where each subject is a separate GCSE.

The exact content, paper structure and practical list depend on the exam board and specification your school uses. Ask your teenager, check their school portal or drop a quick note to their science teacher to confirm both the route and the specification. That single check saves hours of revising topics that are not on their course or missing ones that are. Our guide on revising from your exam specification explains how students use that document as a checklist.

Ask them to explain a process out loud

One of the most effective ways to help your child with GCSE science needs no scientific knowledge from you at all: ask them to teach you.

Pick one short topic they have been revising and say, "Talk me through how this works as if I have never heard of it." When a teenager has to explain a process in plain English, two useful things happen. First, they realise straight away which steps they actually understand. Second, any gap shows up immediately, because they stall or reach for their notes.

You do not need to judge whether their science is accurate. Your job is to ask honest, curious questions whenever something is unclear:

  • "Why does that step happen next?"
  • "What does that word mean in everyday language?"
  • "Could you draw a quick sketch to show me?"
  • "What would happen if you changed that part?"

Keep it relaxed and short. Five minutes at the dining table or while making dinner is plenty. If they get stuck on a step, resist the urge to search for an answer yourself. Simply say, "Good catch, let's look at your notes together to see how your teacher explained it."

Use their own revision cards and teacher materials

You do not need to buy a pile of new books or build a science fact bank at home. Your teenager's exercise books, revision cards, teacher handouts and school revision lists already match their exact course.

If they have flashcards, offer to test them for ten minutes. Read the prompt on the front and let them answer out loud before you check the back. When they get one right, put it in one pile; when they hesitate or miss a key word, put it in a second pile to revisit the next day.

Scientific wording matters in GCSE science, so pay attention to the exact key terms on their card. You do not need to know what the term means yourself to spot whether they used it. If their answer is close but misses a word written on the back, you can say, "You got the idea, and your card also uses this word. Try saying it again with that in."

Practise reading diagrams and graphs together

Science papers regularly ask students to describe what a diagram, table or graph shows. This is another area where a parent can help without subject expertise.

Sit beside your teenager with a diagram or graph from their workbook and ask them to talk you through it:

  • What is on each axis, and what are the units?
  • What is the overall pattern as one side goes up?
  • Is there a point where the pattern changes?
  • What is the diagram trying to show in one sentence?

Asking your child to point to the exact part of the graph that backs up what they said builds a habit of looking closely at the evidence on the page rather than guessing from memory.

Keep calculations calm and step by step

Maths shows up across all three sciences, especially in physics and chemistry. If your child freezes at a calculation, you can still help by slowing the question down.

Ask them three simple questions:

  1. What numbers has the question given you?
  2. What is it asking you to find?
  3. What are the units at the end?

Often, writing the given values down neatly is enough to unstick a student who felt overwhelmed by a wall of text. If they are unsure which formula to use, ask them to check their own specification's equation list or their teacher's notes, because what is given in the exam varies by board and year. For more general ways to support number work at home, see our guide on helping your child with GCSE maths.

Support short, steady sessions rather than long marathons

GCSE science covers a lot of ground across three subjects, so it is easy for a teenager to feel that revision has to be a huge, exhausting block. Short, regular sessions work far better.

You can help by agreeing a simple routine: twenty or twenty-five minutes on one small topic, followed by a proper break. Rotate between biology, chemistry and physics across the week so the subject they find hardest does not quietly get pushed to the bottom of the pile.

Notice effort rather than scores. Saying "You stuck with that topic even though it was tricky" keeps momentum going far better than asking what mark they got. If you want similar ideas for humanities, our post on helping your child with GCSE English Literature uses the same no-subject-knowledge approach.

When to bring the teacher in

Sometimes a teenager is genuinely stuck on a concept and their notes do not clear it up. That is a normal part of Year 11, not a sign that either of you has failed.

Help them turn a vague worry like "I don't get chemistry" into one specific question they can take to their teacher, such as "Can you show me how to balance this type of equation again?" Teachers can help much faster when a student arrives with one clear question.

A calm way to help your child with GCSE science

Your teenager already has science teachers at school. What they need from you is simpler and just as valuable: a steady routine, a friendly listener while they explain a process out loud, and someone who helps them check their own notes without turning the dining table into a classroom. Start with one five-minute explanation this week and let them lead.
