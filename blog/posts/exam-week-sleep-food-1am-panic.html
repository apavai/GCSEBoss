Supporting Your Teenager Through GCSE Exam Week: Sleep, Food, and the 1am Panic

It's half-term. The papers are paused, not finished. And at some point this week your Year 11 will appear in the kitchen at 1am convinced they haven't done enough. Here's what that's actually about — and what to do.

If you're working out how to be useful when supporting teenager exam week looks like a week that's somehow both relaxed and not, you're in the right place. The May half-term lands roughly in the middle of the GCSE exam window — Week 4 of 8 for most boards — and it's a strange week. Exams are paused. Most of the hard ones are still to come. The house is busier than usual. And the 1am kitchen visit is almost guaranteed to happen at least once.

The advice that floats around at this point in the calendar tends to fall into two unhelpful camps. One says back off completely and trust them. The other says draw up a half-term revision timetable and supervise it. Neither lands for the moment your teenager is in. What follows is what we hear actually works from the parents and teachers we talk to — small, specific, and almost entirely about sleep, food, and presence.

The 1am "I haven't done enough" panic is almost never about revision

The first time it happens it feels like an emergency. Your teenager is downstairs in pyjamas, the kettle is on, and they're convinced they're going to fail Paper 2 because they don't really understand enzyme inhibition or quadratic sequences or whichever topic is unlucky enough to surface that night. The instinct is to either panic with them or fix the topic on the spot.

Neither is the move. What's actually happening at 1am is that the cumulative tiredness of three weeks of exams has run their stress system out of reserves. The topic is the lid that came off — it isn't the contents of the pot. A teenager who has slept seven hours for three nights in a row almost never has a 1am panic episode. A teenager running on five hours for the same period almost always does. The intervention is sleep, not study.

What to actually say: "You've done the work. Tonight your brain just needs to switch off. Have some water, come back to bed, and we'll look at it in the morning if it's still bothering you." Then — crucially — do not get the textbook out. Do not test them on it. Do not ask what they've covered today. The job in that moment is to be a calm, slightly boring presence that returns the house to night-time.

Defend the sleep harder than they will

The single most-replicated finding in the memory-consolidation literature is that sleep is when the brain moves information from short-term to long-term storage. Tes's parent-revision guidance — and most of what teachers will quietly tell you — converges on the same point: a rested brain remembers more than a tired one, every single time. The last two hours of revision are worth less than the last two hours of sleep.

Your teenager will not enforce this on themselves. Their internal clock is naturally pushed about two hours later than yours at 15–16, which means 11pm feels to them roughly what 9pm feels to you. Add exam adrenaline and the phone, and the desk light stays on until 1am unless someone in the house gently puts a stop to it.

A useful framing: half-term week is the moment to reset the sleep clock for the second half of the exam window. Aim for lights-out by 11pm Sunday, 10:30 Monday, 10 by Tuesday. Most teenagers will resist the framing and quietly comply with the deadline.

Food: keep it normal, keep it visible, don't make it weird

Exam-week food advice gets over-engineered very quickly. There isn't a magic breakfast. There is, however, a real cost to skipping meals during exam week — blood sugar dips correlate noticeably with both irritability and the late-evening panic loops we just talked about. The thing that helps is having food in the house that they actually eat, in places they'll see it, without anyone making a thing of it.

Practically: leave snacks out on the counter mid-afternoon rather than asking if they want one. Stock the fridge with whatever they reach for normally — yogurts, leftover pasta, the cheese they like. Keep a jug of water in their line of sight on the kitchen table. Hydration is the single biggest unforced error of exam week: most teenagers under-drink by a litre a day during May.

Avoid the special exam breakfast. Avoid the "let me make you something proper". The most useful exam-morning kitchen is the one that looks exactly like every other morning — quiet, normal, predictable. They're already managing enough novelty.

Supporting teenager exam week: half-term is for resetting, not for catching up

The temptation during the half-term pause is to use it to "really get on top of things". This rarely works. Six days of intensive revision is far more likely to leave them flatter and more anxious going back into Monday's paper than four days of mixed revision and rest. Diminishing returns hit fast in the second week of an exam window.

A pattern that works better: two to three hours of focused revision a day, ideally in the morning when their brain is fresh, then the rest of the day genuinely off. Walk, friends, screens, whatever. The body needs the actual downtime — and so does the part of the brain that consolidates what they've already learned. Rest isn't the opposite of revision. It's part of it.

If they want to do more than that, the most useful kind of "more" is past papers under timed conditions rather than re-reading notes. But that's their call. The half-term timetable is theirs to set; your job is to make the conditions for it possible, not to write it.

What to stop doing this week

Stop asking how it's going. Stop asking what they've revised today. Stop asking how they think the last paper went. Stop telling them you remember when you sat your O-levels. Stop forwarding articles about exam stress to the family WhatsApp. Stop telling them your colleague's child is doing eight A*s.

None of this is meant unkindly. All of it lands as pressure. The most useful thing you can probably say all week is some version of: "Whatever you need, just shout. I'm around."

Real visibility, without the drama

One of the reasons we built the parent dashboard inside GCSE Boss is precisely this moment. The alternative to asking your teenager every evening whether they've revised — which wrecks the relationship and doesn't even give you accurate data — is having quiet visibility on what they've actually covered, by topic, this week. Real visibility, without the drama. You see what's happening without having to ask.

It isn't a tool for half-term week specifically. It's a tool for not needing the 1am kitchen conversation to be the moment you find out they haven't looked at chemistry yet. By the time the panic surfaces, the information you needed has been visible for days.

One last thing

Three weeks more. That's all it is now. The exams resume Monday and the last paper for most boards is in the week of 22 June. You are nearly through the hardest stretch of the school calendar, and you've been useful in the ways that count — by being the person in the house who keeps the kitchen normal and the bedtime non-negotiable.

Make the tea. Stock the fridge. Defend the sleep. When the 1am visit happens, send them back to bed with water and a calm sentence. They'll be fine.
