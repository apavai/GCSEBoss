GCSE Revision That Follows Your Exam Spec

You can spend a whole Sunday on GCSE revision and still walk into a paper that does not look like the one you practised. The list is usually the problem.

GCSE revision gets treated like a pile of topics with the same name as your subject. Biology is biology, maths is maths, and a tidy checklist feels like a plan. Then a question lands on a required practical your class never did, or skips a whole optional unit you spent two evenings on, and the hours look busier than they were useful.

That is a map problem. Your exam is one specification, with a board, a code, a tier, and a short list of optional units. A generic list does not know any of that.

Why generic-list GCSE revision wastes an evening

A generic list is built for everyone, so it has to hedge. It includes topics your board dropped. It skips the case studies your teacher picked.

You then do the comfortable thing: start at the top and work down. The top of a generic list is usually the first chapter of a textbook, not the spec point you still cannot produce from a blank page. You finish the evening having covered something. You have not practised the paper.

The giveaway is the feeling of "I have seen this" followed by a blank under timing. You have seen a version of the topic. You have not practised the version your board asks for, in the wording they use.

Find your exam spec before you make another list

You need four facts per subject, written down once.

  • The exam board. In the UK that is one of AQA, Pearson Edexcel, OCR, WJEC/Eduqas or CCEA. Your school can use different boards for different subjects, so do not assume last year's English board is this year's history board.
  • The specification code. It looks boring and it is the useful bit. Something like 8461 or 1MA1. Two courses can share a subject name and still be different specifications. The code is how you tell them apart.
  • The tier, if the subject has one. Foundation and Higher are different papers. Some content only appears on Higher. Putting those topics on a Foundation list is a long walk to a question that will not be printed.
  • The optional units and set texts. History depth studies, geography case studies, English Literature texts, the language you actually take. A generic list cannot know which ones your class does. Your teacher, your exercise book, or the spec plus the school's options sheet can.

Where the four facts live: the front of the textbook, the course page on the school VLE, the header of a mock paper, or a two-line message to the teacher. If you already have a paper from this course, read the header before you open a new checklist.

Write them in one note. You will reuse this for every subject.

What on spec actually means

On spec is not "a topic with a similar name". It is the published specification point, in the wording the board uses.

"Cell structure" on a generic list might add detail your paper never asks for, and miss the required practical or the maths skill that sits next to it on your spec. "Algebra" on one board can mean rearranging. On another it leans into proof. The chapter title is a nickname. The spec point is the job.

Open the specification PDF for your code. The contents page is the real list. If a line is not there, it is not in the exam, however often it shows up online. If a line is there and your class has not reached it yet, it still belongs on the long list. It does not belong on this week's.

This is also where Combined Science trips people. Combined is its own specification, with its own codes and its own cut of the content. Using a Triple list for Combined (or the other way round) is one of the most common ways to spend October on the wrong pages.

Turn the spec into a list you can work

Once you have the PDF, do not rewrite it in nicer handwriting. Use it.

Go through the spec points for one subject and mark each one as: I can do this from a blank page, I sort of know it, I have not been taught it yet. Be honest about the middle pile. "Sort of" is the pile that feels finished and then disappears in a mock.

For the ones you can already produce, leave them alone except for a short retrieval pass later. For the middle pile, practise the spec wording, not a summary. Close the notes, write what the point is asking, then check. For the ones you have not been taught, keep them on the long list. A video is not a substitute for the lesson you have not had.

Retrieval beats rereading. If you cannot produce the point from memory, it was reviewed, it was not revised.

Do one subject properly before you colour-code six. A complete spec list for one course is worth more than a pretty grid for everything.

What to do with this week

Late August is a good moment for this. You are collecting the map.

Three jobs, and then stop.

  • Confirm board, spec code and tier for every GCSE you are sitting. If you cannot find one, ask. Guessing is how people follow the neighbouring specification until half term.
  • Pick one subject and walk the contents page of the real spec. Mark the three piles.
  • Leave the new stationery. A correct list in Notes beats a new folder with the wrong topics on the dividers.

When a list is lying to you

The list has no board name and no spec code on it. It is a subject list. It is not yours. It treats all science as the same course. It includes a topic your teacher has already said is not on your option. It is organised by textbook chapter, and nobody has checked the chapter against the spec. Or it belonged to someone at a different school, with different texts.

None of those make you lazy. They make the hours harder to trust. Swap the list. Keep the hours.

Revising from the spec

Once the list is yours, the work gets simpler. You stop deciding what counts as revision. You work the next spec point you cannot produce.

Short sessions. One point, from memory, then check. If you got it, tick and move. If you did not, that point is the next session. It is not a reason to reread the whole chapter.

Past papers come after that. They test whether a spec point survives timing. They are a poor way to discover the spec for the first time, because you only see the points that happened to come up that year.
