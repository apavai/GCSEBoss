GCSE Exam Timing: Practising When to Move On From a Question

GCSE exam timing is less about writing fast and more about deciding well. Most students who run short of time in a paper are not slow writers. They spend too long on one question that is not going their way, and the questions after it get squeezed. The good news is that knowing when to move on is a skill, and like any skill you can practise it before your mocks.

This guide is about rehearsing that decision calmly, on a short section, so it feels familiar by the time you sit a full paper.

Start with the real paper, not a rule

You may have heard simple rules about how many minutes to spend per mark. They can be a rough guide, but papers are different. Some have a reading section, some include long writing tasks, some expect you to check calculations, and the total time and number of marks vary by subject and paper.

So before you practise, look at the front of a real paper your teacher has given you, or one from your school's past papers. Note three things:

  1. How long the paper lasts.
  2. How many marks it is out of, and how the marks are split between sections.
  3. Any instructions about which questions to answer, choices between questions, or reading time.

From that, you can sketch a rough plan, such as roughly where you would like to be by halfway. Keep it loose. The point is to have a sense of pace, not a stopwatch for every question.

If you are not sure how to divide the time for a particular subject, ask your teacher. They know the paper, and many will have advice for that specific exam.

Why moving on is a good decision

Leaving a question can feel like giving up. It is not. Moving on is a choice to come back later with fresh eyes, once you have collected the marks you can get elsewhere.

There are a few good reasons to move on:

  • You have read the question twice and still do not know where to start.
  • You have been stuck on the same step for a while and are going round in circles.
  • You are well past the time you planned for that question.

When that happens, a quick, honest decision is better than another few minutes of staring at the same line.

How to mark a question so you come back

The move-on habit works best when coming back is easy. Agree a small system with yourself and use it every time you practise.

  • Mark it clearly. Put a small symbol next to the question number, such as a star or a circle, so you can spot it when you flick back.
  • Leave a short note. If you got part of the way, jot down the next step you were thinking of. Two or three words is enough.
  • Leave space. If you are writing in an answer booklet, leave room to add to your answer later rather than squeezing it in.
  • Write something useful first. On longer questions, a first point or a first line of working may earn credit even if you do not finish. Your teacher can tell you how marks are given in each subject.

Before you hand in a paper, look for your symbols. That is where your remaining time should go.

GCSE exam timing practice: a short section, not a full paper

You do not need to sit a full paper every time you practise timing. In fact, it is often more useful to practise on a short section first.

Here is a simple way to do it:

  1. Pick one section of a paper, perhaps five or six questions.
  2. Work out a fair time for it from the paper's total time and marks, or ask your teacher for a sensible stopping point.
  3. Set a plain timer and put it where you can see it without picking up your phone.
  4. Work through the section, and practise moving on from at least one question when it stalls, even if you think you might get there.
  5. When the time ends, stop. Then go back to any marked questions with a few extra minutes if you have them.

Afterwards, mark it with the mark scheme. Our guide on using a mark scheme explains how to read it properly rather than just checking the final answer.

Look at what the timing tells you

The most useful part of timed practice is what you notice afterwards. Ask yourself:

  • Which question took the most time, and was it worth that many marks?
  • Did I move on when I meant to, or did I hang on too long?
  • When I came back to a marked question, could I do more with it?
  • Did I leave any time to read through and check?

Write one line about it. A simple GCSE error log is a good place to keep these notes, so you can see patterns over a few weeks. Maybe long-answer questions always run over, or you rush the first page and then slow down. Once you know the pattern, you can practise exactly that.

Leave time to check

Planning a few minutes at the end to read back through is part of good timing, not an extra. Checking lets you spot a missed question, a unit you forgot or a sentence that does not quite say what you meant. When you plan your pace, build that time in from the start rather than hoping some will be left over.

Extra time and other arrangements

Some students have access arrangements, such as extra time or rest breaks. These are decided and confirmed by your school, not something you can assume. If you have them, ask your teacher or the school's exams team how they apply to your mocks, and practise with the time you will actually have. If you think you might need support, talk to your teacher or form tutor.

Building the habit before mocks

Like anything else, timing gets easier with repetition. Try a short timed section once or twice a week alongside your normal revision. Over time, the decision to move on stops feeling like a big moment and becomes a normal part of how you work through a paper. When you are ready for full papers, our guide to GCSE past papers has ideas on using them well.

Remember that nobody gets every question first time. Good exam timing is simply making sure you have had a fair go at all of them.
