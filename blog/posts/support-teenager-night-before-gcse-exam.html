How to Support Your Teenager the Night Before a GCSE Exam

Your teenager has a paper in the morning. They're stressed. You want to help but you've been told to back off. Here's what actually works, and what to stop doing.

If you're trying to work out how to support your GCSE child during exams, you'll get a lot of conflicting advice. Some of it tells you to be hands-off and let them get on with it. Some of it tells you to hover, supervise, ask about every topic. Neither is right. Both are exhausting.

The reality is simpler. The night before an exam is a different job than supporting them through the rest of revision. Most of what you'd normally do (asking what they've covered, suggesting topics, offering to test them) actively gets in the way tonight. What they need from you the night before is a small, specific set of things that nobody warns you about.

How to support your GCSE child during exams: the night-before list

We've talked to a lot of parents and a lot of teachers over the last few years, and the same pattern comes up. The most useful parents the night before an exam do less, not more. The ones who do the most damage are usually the ones trying hardest.

Here's what actually helps.

Stop asking if they've revised enough

They have, or they haven't. Asking now changes nothing. What it does do is plant doubt in their head ten hours before they need to walk into the room thinking they've got this. The question is for your benefit, not theirs.

If you can replace one thing in your repertoire tonight, replace "have you done enough?" with "you've done the work. Tonight is about landing it." It's the single most useful sentence you can hand a teenager the night before an exam.

Defend their sleep harder than they will

Sleep is when their brain moves what they've learned from short-term memory into long-term memory. A teenager who got eight hours on 70% prep almost always outperforms one who got four hours on 90% prep. This isn't pop science. It's the most-replicated finding in the entire memory-consolidation literature.

Your job tonight is to be the person who notices when they're still at the desk at 11:30pm and gently shuts it down. They will not do this for themselves. They'll convince themselves the next 90 minutes are worth losing two hours of sleep over. They aren't. The last two hours of revision are worth less than the last two hours of sleep, every time.

Sort the practical stuff so they don't have to in the morning

Pens: two black biros, not one. Calculator with fresh batteries if it's a maths or science paper. Water bottle. Watch (their phone won't be allowed in the room). The right uniform. Their candidate number if they can't remember it. ID, if your school asks for it.

None of this looks like revision. All of it prevents the 7:45am panic that wipes out whatever calm they built up overnight. Doing it as a quiet job after they've gone to bed is the single highest-leverage 10 minutes you'll spend in the whole GCSE period.

Make the kitchen feel normal in the morning

Toast, juice, water, whatever they actually eat. Not a special exam breakfast. Not "are you sure you don't want eggs?" They're 15, they don't want eggs at 7:30am. The thing that messes teenagers up most on exam morning isn't the exam, it's anyone in the house treating the morning as anything other than a normal morning.

The most useful thing you can do at breakfast is exist quietly in the same room, hand them whatever they reach for, and not ask any questions starting with "are you feeling…?"

What not to do

Don't quiz them. Don't ask about topics. Don't offer to "just go through" anything. Don't share war stories about exams you sat in 1993. Don't tell them about your colleague's child who's doing eight A*s. Don't, for the love of god, post on the family WhatsApp at 10pm that "tomorrow is the big day".

If you want to send a text, send it the morning of the exam and make it short: "You've got this. See you after." That's the whole message. Anything longer is for you, not them.

The parent dashboard, while we're here

One of the reasons we built the parent dashboard inside GCSE Boss is that the alternative (asking your teenager every evening whether they've revised) wrecks the relationship and doesn't even give you accurate data. Real visibility, without the drama. You see what's happening without having to ask. That's the whole brief.

Tonight isn't the night to start using it. But if you find yourself standing in the kitchen tomorrow wondering whether they actually opened a book this week, that's what it's for.

One last thing

You're doing the right thing by reading this. Most parents don't bother. The fact that you're thinking about how to be useful tonight, rather than how to dig in and revise alongside them, is itself the answer.

Make the tea. Sort the pens. Defend the sleep. Send the short text in the morning. They'll be fine.
