"I Didn't Get the GCSE Grades I Needed" — What to Do Next, Calmly

If you didn't get the GCSE grades you needed, read this before you do anything else. The short version: you have more options than it feels like right now, and none of them have to be sorted in the first hour.

Results land on Thursday 20 August 2026. If you're reading this before then, you're bracing for a number that might not be the one you wanted. And if you're reading it on the day, and the grades weren't what you needed — first, breathe. This is one of those moments that feels enormous and permanent, and it is genuinely neither. Thousands of people open an envelope every August, find a grade they didn't expect, and go on to do exactly what they wanted anyway, just by a slightly different route.

So this isn't a pep talk that pretends everything is fine. If you didn't get the GCSE grades you needed, that's a real thing and it's allowed to sting. It's a guide to what actually happens next — the specific, boring, useful steps — so the disappointment doesn't turn into a decision you make too fast.

Didn't get the GCSE grades you needed? Nothing has to be decided today

Here's the single most important line in this whole post: results day is for information, not for big decisions. The grades are already set. Nothing you choose in the first hour changes them, and nothing gets locked shut if you don't act by lunchtime. Your college place, your resit options, a review of a paper — they're all still there tomorrow, and most of them are there next week too.

So give yourself the morning. Get the actual grades in front of you, tell someone you trust, and let the first wave of feeling pass before you start planning. Decisions made at 8am on no sleep are almost always worse than the same decisions made after a cup of tea and a proper conversation.

Get clear on what actually happened

Before you plan anything, look at the detail. Which subjects came in where? Was it one grade that fell short of what you needed, or a few? Were any of them close — a mark or two off a boundary rather than a whole grade below? That matters, because the "close" ones and the "well below" ones have different sensible next steps.

You'll get your grades from your school or college, and from summer 2026 students in England can also view them digitally through the new Education Record app — useful if you can't get in to collect a paper slip. Grade boundaries for each board are published at 8am on results day, so if you want to see how close a paper was to the next grade up, that information is public by the time you open your results.

If you were close: ask about a review of marking

If a grade is only just below what you needed, it's worth asking your school about a review of marking (sometimes called a remark). This is where an examiner re-checks that your paper was marked correctly. It won't help every case, and a review can leave a grade the same or, occasionally, move it down as well as up — so it's a considered step, not an automatic one. The key thing to know: these requests go through your school or college, not from you directly, and each exam board has a deadline. If you think a review might be worth it, raise it with school quickly so you don't miss the window — but you still don't have to decide the instant you get your results.

Resits: the November 2026 route

If you need to improve a grade, you can resit. For the November series specifically, the resits on offer are English Language and Maths GCSEs only — the two subjects most colleges and employers care most about. The JCQ common exam timetable for the November 2026 series runs from 2 to 18 November 2026, and those results come out on 14 January 2027. Other subjects are resat in next summer's series rather than in November.

A resit isn't starting from scratch. You've already sat the course once, so you know the shape of it — you're topping up the bits that cost you marks, not relearning everything. Talk to your school about entering for November before you commit — they organise the entry.

Talking to your school or college

Whatever you're weighing up — a review, a resit, or a change of plan — the fastest way to sort it is a proper conversation with the college or sixth form you're heading to. If you were close to their entry requirements, that isn't automatically a closed door: places shift on results day, some courses have a bit of flex, and admissions staff have this exact conversation dozens of times every August. Ask them plainly what your options are. You'll almost always get a clearer, calmer answer than the worst-case version running in your head.

If college feels wrong now, that's information too. There are other routes — different courses, apprenticeships, resitting alongside your other studies — and none of them are a lesser version of a life. Ask about them properly before you rule anything in or out.

Keeping it in perspective (and where to get support)

For most people, a disappointing results day is genuinely a bump, not a wall — a few weeks of feeling flat, a plan that shifts a little, and then normal life. But if this is hitting harder than that — if you can't sleep, you feel hopeless, or you're pulling away from everyone — please tell someone. A parent, a teacher, your GP, or someone confidential: Childline (0800 1111) is free and open 24/7 for anyone under 19, and Samaritans (116 123) is there any time of day or night. Reaching out early isn't a sign anything has gone wrong with you — it's just a sensible thing to do when a day feels heavy.

Where your grades come from

One last bit of context that keeps things in proportion: your grades were set by the exam boards and are simply passed on by your school on results day. Either way: get the grades in front of you, talk to people who know the options, and leave the big decisions until after the first feeling has passed. You didn't get the GCSE grades you needed today — and you've still got a clear, unhurried set of next steps. Take them one at a time.
