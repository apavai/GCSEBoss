How to Motivate a Teenager to Revise: Setting Targets You Both Agree On

If you've ever tried to work out how to motivate a teenager to revise, you'll know the standard playbook — a reward dangled in exchange for hours at the desk — has a habit of turning into a negotiation neither of you enjoys. There's a calmer version, and it starts with a target you both actually agreed to.

It's the middle of a quiet summer, results are still five weeks off, and the year ahead is the sort of thing that's easy to think about now and much harder to think about in November. Which makes this a surprisingly good moment to sort out the one thing that quietly decides whether revision happens at all next year: motivation. Not the fireworks kind — the boring, durable kind that gets someone to the desk on an ordinary Tuesday.

The instinct, when a teenager won't get going, is to reach for a reward. Finish the chapter and you can have the controller back. It works, sort of, for about a week, and then it curdles into a haggle where the price keeps going up and the work keeps shrinking. The problem isn't rewards themselves. It's who set the terms.

Why the bribery battle backfires

When a reward is handed down — do this, get that — it puts you in charge of the goal and your teenager in the position of doing the minimum to collect. You've accidentally made revision your project, not theirs. And a fifteen or sixteen-year-old is developmentally wired to push back on things imposed from above; that's not defiance for its own sake, it's the ordinary business of becoming an adult who makes their own decisions.

There's a well-established distinction in the motivation research between doing something because you've genuinely bought into it and doing it purely to earn an external prize. The second kind is fragile: it works only while the prize is on offer, and it can actually dampen the internal interest that keeps people going once the prize disappears. That doesn't mean rewards are bad. It means the reward works far better when it's attached to a goal your teenager helped set, rather than one they were handed.

Source: Deci, Koestner & Ryan, "A meta-analytic review of experiments examining the effects of extrinsic rewards on intrinsic motivation," Psychological Bulletin, 1999.

How to motivate a teenager to revise: start with a target, not a reward

The reorder is small but it changes everything. Instead of opening with "if you revise, you can have X," open with a question: what would a good week actually look like? Not a heroic week — a realistic one. Three sessions? Four? A particular subject that's been avoided? Let them do most of the talking, and resist the urge to double whatever number they suggest.

A target your teenager proposed is one they can't easily disown later. If they said three revision sessions this week, the follow-up conversation isn't a lecture, it's a simple "how did we do against the three?" — a shared scoreboard rather than a verdict you're delivering. The word "we" matters here. You're on the same side of the table, both looking at the same goal, which is exactly the tone the summer lets you set before the pressure arrives.

Now add the reward — attached to the target you agreed

This is where a reward earns its place. Once the target exists and it's genuinely theirs, you can both decide what hitting it is worth. Maybe it's a trip, a game, a later bedtime at the weekend, money towards something they're saving for. The key is that the reward is tied to the agreed target, not to a running tally of hours or to your moment-to-moment sense of whether they've done enough.

A few things make rewards work rather than backfire. Keep them proportionate — a reward wildly out of scale with the effort teaches them to hold out for more. Tie them to effort you can both see, like "did the four sessions," not to outcomes neither of you controls. And honour them without renegotiating; the whole point is that the deal was fair because you both built it, so moving the goalposts afterwards quietly poisons the next one.

What "targets you both agree on" looks like in practice

Concretely: on a Sunday, you spend ten minutes together. They pick the subjects that need the most attention and the number of sessions that feels doable. You agree what a good week is worth. Then you both leave it alone until the following Sunday, when you look back at how it went — calmly, without a running commentary each evening in between.

The revision itself is theirs to run. The nightly "have you done your revision?" is exactly the hovering that turns a shared goal back into your project. If you've agreed the target on Sunday, you've already had the useful conversation; the other six days you can mostly let go of, which is better for the work and considerably better for the house.

When motivation genuinely won't come

Some weeks the target gets missed, and it's worth saying plainly that this is normal and not a character flaw. Motivation is genuinely hard, especially for a teenager juggling nine subjects, a social life, and a brain that's still building the machinery for long-term planning. A missed week is information, not a crime — usually it means the target was too big, or the subject was one they're quietly stuck on and avoiding.

The useful response to a missed target is curiosity, not consequence. "That one didn't happen — was it too much, or was it the subject?" tends to get you somewhere. A withdrawn privilege usually just confirms their sense that this is a battle to be endured. Keep the door open, shrink the target if you need to, and start again next Sunday.
