GCSE History Source Questions: How to Read a Source and Say Something Useful

GCSE history source questions can feel strange at first. You're handed a picture, a poster or a few lines of text, and you're expected to say something clever about it. The good news is that reading a source is a skill, not a talent. Once you have a simple routine, every source starts to feel less like a puzzle and more like evidence you can question.

This guide gives you a routine for GCSE history source questions. It works whatever period or topic your class is studying, because it's about how historians think, not about one particular set of facts.

What GCSE history source questions are really asking

Underneath the wording, most source work comes down to a few big ideas:

  • What does this source show or tell us?
  • Who made it, when, and why?
  • How far can we trust it, and for what?
  • What does it leave out?

If you can answer those four questions clearly, you have the raw material for almost anything your teacher asks. Your teacher will explain exactly how your course words its questions and what a strong answer looks like, so always check their guidance. What follows is the thinking that sits underneath.

How to analyse a history source in four steps

Step 1: Look before you judge

Start by simply noticing. What can you actually see or read? If it's a picture, who's in it, what are they doing, and what's in the background? If it's text, what is the writer describing or arguing?

Jot down three or four plain observations. Don't jump to opinions yet. Lots of students lose good points because they rush past what's right in front of them.

Step 2: Read the caption like a detective

The small print under a source is often the most useful part. It might tell you who made it, when, where it was published, or who it was meant for. These details are called the source's origin, or provenance.

Ask yourself:

  • Who made it? A politician, a soldier, a journalist, an artist, an ordinary person?
  • When? At the time of the events, or years later looking back?
  • Where was it published, and for whom? A newspaper, a private diary, a government poster?

Each answer gives you something to say. A private diary might be more honest about feelings. A public poster was made to persuade people. Neither is "good" or "bad"; each is useful for different things.

Step 3: Ask why it was made

This is where your answer starts to sound like a historian's. Every source was created for a reason: to inform, to persuade, to record, to entertain, to criticise or to remember.

Think about purpose and audience together. A cartoon in a newspaper that opposed a government is likely to make that government look foolish. That doesn't make the cartoon useless. It makes it very useful for showing what its critics thought.

A helpful sentence starter is: "The author's purpose was probably to... so the source is especially useful for showing..."

Step 4: Check it against what you know

Now bring in your own knowledge. Does the source match what you've learned about the period? Does it exaggerate, miss things out, or show only one side?

This is often the difference between a description and an analysis. Saying "the source shows a crowded street" is a start. Saying "the source shows a crowded street, which fits what we know about rapid town growth at the time, but it doesn't show the poor living conditions described in other evidence" is much stronger.

GCSE history source analysis: a worked example

Here's a short practice source. It's invented for this guide, not a real historical document, so you can focus on the method.

Made-up example: "Our village celebrated the new railway with music and flags. Everyone agrees it will bring work and good fortune." From a speech by the local landowner who invested in the railway, printed in the town newspaper the week it opened.

Using the four steps:

  • Look: the speaker describes a happy celebration and says everyone supports the railway.
  • Caption: it's a speech by a landowner who invested money, printed in the local paper at the time.
  • Why: he has a reason to make the railway sound popular and successful, because he wants it to succeed and people to use it.
  • Check: your own knowledge might tell you that new railways brought jobs for some people but also disruption, and not everyone welcomed them. "Everyone agrees" is a big claim.

So you might write: the source is useful for showing how supporters of the railway wanted it to be seen, but less useful for showing what all villagers really thought, because the speaker had a strong reason to present it positively.

That's a balanced, evidence-based point, and it came straight from asking simple questions.

Common mistakes to avoid

  • Just describing. Saying what's in the source is only the first step. Always go on to ask who, why and how far you can trust it.
  • Calling a source "biased" and stopping. Every source has a point of view. The useful question is what that point of view lets the source show you.
  • Ignoring the caption. The origin details are often where the best points come from.
  • Forgetting your own knowledge. Linking the source to what you've learned in class shows real understanding.

How to practise source skills

You don't need a full practice paper to get better. Try these little and often:

  • One source a day. Pick a picture or short extract from your class notes or textbook and run through the four steps in five minutes.
  • Say it out loud. Explain to a friend or family member who made the source and why. If you can explain it, you understand it.
  • Swap the author. Ask how the same event might look if someone on the other side had described it.
  • Link to people and events. Knowing the key people in your topic makes origin details much easier to use. Our guide to GCSE history revision with named people can help with that.

Testing yourself like this is a form of active recall, which you can read more about in our guide to active recall for GCSE revision.

The more sources you question, the more natural it becomes. Before long, you'll pick up a source and the questions will come to you automatically: who made it, why, and what it can really tell you.
