What Grades Do You Need for Sixth Form? (And What Happens If You're Close)

With results landing on Thursday 20 August 2026, plenty of families are quietly asking the same thing: what grades do you need for sixth form, and what happens if your teen lands just short? Here's the calm, general picture, and why "close" is often more flexible than it feels the night before.

In the run-up to results day, plenty of parents are doing the maths in their head: matching the grades their teen is hoping for against the entry requirements of the sixth form or college they've got their eye on. It's a sensible thing to look into now, while it's calm, rather than at 8am on the day with an envelope in hand.

The honest headline is that there's no single national answer. Entry requirements are set by each individual school sixth form or college, so the only ones that truly matter are those for your teen's chosen place and course. What follows is the common shape of things (the numbers that come up again and again) so you know what to look for and what to ask.

What grades do you need for sixth form? The general picture

When people ask what grades you need for sixth form, they're usually really asking about two separate bars: a general entry requirement to get onto the sixth form at all, and a subject-specific requirement for each A-Level or course a student wants to take. Most places set both, and it's worth pulling them apart because a teen can comfortably clear one while being borderline on the other.

A very common general baseline is five GCSEs at grade 4 or above, including English and Maths. Some sixth forms frame it as five grade 9–4 passes; others ask for a certain number at grade 5 or higher. Competitive or academically selective sixth forms may set the bar higher still. The wording varies, but "five including English and Maths" is the pattern you'll see most often as the entry-level requirement.

The five-including-English-and-Maths baseline, explained

Why English and Maths specifically? Because they're the two subjects nearly every next step cares about (further study, apprenticeships and employers alike) so most institutions make them a condition of entry. If a teen is on track for a grade 4 (a standard pass) or grade 5 (a strong pass) in both, they've usually met the part of the requirement that trips people up most often.

Everything else in that "five GCSEs" count tends to be more flexible about which subjects make up the number. The thing to check on any prospectus is whether the sixth form counts a grade 4 or insists on a grade 5 for English and Maths. That single detail is where a lot of "am I in?" questions actually turn.

Subject-specific requirements (and why "a 5" comes up so often)

On top of the general bar, individual A-Levels usually set their own entry grade: most often in the GCSE subject closest to them. A-Level Biology, Chemistry or Physics commonly asks for a grade 5 or 6 in the relevant sciences (and often Maths); A-Level Maths frequently wants a 6 or 7 in GCSE Maths; essay subjects tend to look for a strong grade in GCSE English. The exact numbers differ a lot between colleges, and the more competitive the course, the higher the likely requirement.

This is the layer that catches families out, because a teen can clear the general five-GCSE bar and still be short of the specific grade for one subject. If that happens, it's rarely the end of the plan: usually just a conversation about whether the college will still take them, or whether a slightly different mix of subjects fits better. Which brings us to the "close" question.

What happens if you're close

Here's the reassuring bit that's easy to lose sight of the night before results: being a grade or two short of an advertised requirement is not automatically a closed door. Published entry requirements are a guide, and admissions staff have this exact conversation dozens of times every August. Places move on results day, some courses have a bit of flex, and a teen who's just below the line on one subject is often still very much in the running.

So if your teen opens their results and finds they're close rather than clear, the useful response isn't panic. It's a phone call. Ring the sixth form or college, explain the grades, and ask plainly what the options are. You'll almost always get a calmer, more specific answer than the worst-case version running in everyone's head. The people on the other end of that call want to fill their courses, and they'd far rather talk it through than turn away a student who's nearly there.

If a grade falls genuinely short: resits and reviews

If a result is well below what a course needs (or a review might be worth it because a paper landed just under a boundary), there are still routes, and none of them need actioning in the first hour. A review of marking goes through the school or college rather than from you directly, and each exam board has a deadline, so it's worth raising quickly if you think it applies. Grade boundaries are published on results morning, which helps you see how close a paper actually was.

If a November resit ends up being part of the plan, the school organises the entry, and the autumn is spent topping up specific weak spots rather than relearning a whole course. How to talk to the sixth form or college

Whatever the results say, the fastest way to turn uncertainty into a plan is a proper conversation with the place your teen is heading to. Have the phone number to hand, know roughly which grades matter for their chosen subjects, and let your teen lead the call where they can. Results arrive from around 8am in person on Thursday 20 August 2026, with digital access through the Education Record app from about 11am, so there's time to make that call once the first wave of feeling has passed.

And if sixth form turns out not to be the right fit after all, that's information rather than failure. Apprenticeships and college courses with different entry routes are all real, respectable paths: worth asking about properly rather than ruling out in a rush.
