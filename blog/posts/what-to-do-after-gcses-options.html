What to Do After GCSEs: The Options Conversation to Have Before Results Day

The exams are nearly done, and a new question is quietly moving up the list: what to do after GCSEs. The options — sixth form, college, or an apprenticeship — don't need deciding this week, but they are worth talking through now, in the calm of the summer, rather than in the rush of results morning. Here's a parent's guide to having that conversation without adding any pressure to it.

There's a particular kind of limbo that sets in once the last paper is in sight. The hard graft of revision is behind your teenager, results day is weeks away, and the obvious next thing — "so, what happens after this?" — hangs in the air without an easy answer. It's tempting to either avoid it entirely or to try to settle it in one big sit-down. Neither is necessary. The most useful thing you can do right now is open the conversation gently, lay the options out plainly, and let it breathe over the summer.

Why talk about what to do after GCSEs options before results day?

The instinct to wait for grades is understandable — surely you can only choose once you know what's on the certificate? In practice, the opposite is true. Before results day there's no number on the table to argue about, no disappointment or relief colouring the decision, and no clock ticking. That makes it the calmest window you'll get to explore what your teenager actually wants. You're not committing to anything; you're just mapping the territory so that when grades do land, the conversation is a continuation rather than a cold start. Most sixth forms, colleges and apprenticeship providers ask for applications or expressions of interest well before results anyway, so a bit of early thinking is rarely wasted.

Route one: sixth form

Sixth form — whether attached to a school or a standalone sixth-form college — is the familiar academic route, usually three or four A-levels (or equivalent Level 3 qualifications) over two years. It tends to suit teenagers who like the structure of a school-style timetable, have subjects they genuinely enjoy, and are leaning towards options that keep university on the table. If your teenager already knows the two or three subjects that light them up, sixth form is often the path of least friction. Worth checking together: which subjects each setting actually offers, the typical entry requirements, and whether staying at their current school or moving somewhere new feels right.

Route two: college

A further education college covers a much wider spread than people sometimes assume — academic courses, yes, but also vocational and technical qualifications, T-levels, and applied courses that mix classroom learning with real workplace skills. College can be the better fit for a teenager who wants something more hands-on, a course tied closely to a career area, or simply a fresh environment with a slightly more adult feel after five years at the same school. It's also a strong option for keeping doors open while specialising — many college courses still lead on to university, an apprenticeship, or employment. The thing to look at is how a given course is assessed and where it leads, so the choice fits where they think they're heading.

Route three: apprenticeship

An apprenticeship combines paid work with training, so your teenager earns while they learn and comes out with a recognised qualification and real experience. It suits someone who's ready to be in a workplace, learns best by doing, and has a sense of the kind of industry they want to be in. The competitive part is that good apprenticeships are applied for like jobs, with deadlines and interviews, so this route rewards a bit of early legwork. It is in no way a "lesser" path — for plenty of teenagers it's the most motivating and direct one. If your teenager raises it, take it as seriously as any other option and look together at what's available locally and online.

The questions worth asking now

You don't need answers yet — you need good questions. A few that tend to open things up without putting your teenager on the spot: which parts of the last two years did you actually enjoy, not just do well at? Do you want to keep studying a range of subjects, or focus on one direction? Does sitting in a classroom still appeal, or would you rather be doing something more practical? Is being in a workplace something you're curious about? Keep it light and genuinely open — the goal is to hear what they think, not to steer them towards the answer you'd pick. Sometimes the most useful thing you can say is simply, "there's no wrong choice here, let's just look at what's out there."

Keeping options open before grades land

The honest reality is that grades will shape what's available, and that's fine to acknowledge calmly. The trick is to keep more than one door open until you know more. That might mean noting the entry requirements for a couple of routes, glancing at what each provider offers, and keeping a loose plan B in mind without dwelling on it. There's no need to game out every scenario — just enough that results day brings a choice between prepared options rather than a scramble. If you've had visibility over your teenager's revision this year and have a rough sense of where their strengths sit, you'll already have a feel for which routes are realistic; if not, the conversations above will get you most of the way there.

Keep it a conversation, not a verdict

Above all, this is a relationship moment, not a careers interview. Your teenager has just spent weeks being measured, and the last thing they need is to feel measured again at the kitchen table. Float the topic, listen more than you talk, and let it sit between chats rather than forcing a conclusion. Whatever they're drawn to — sixth form, college, or an apprenticeship — treat all three as equally valid, because they are. Decide nothing today. The win this week is simply that the question of what to do after GCSEs is now out in the open, talked about without pressure, and ready to revisit when the grades arrive. That's the calm start that makes results day so much easier.
