GCSE Business Revision: Learning Case Studies You Can Actually Use in the Exam

GCSE business revision goes wrong in a quiet way. You read the textbook chapter, you nod along, and then a question gives you a business you have never heard of and asks you to recommend what it should do. The facts you learned sit in your head with nowhere to go. The fix is not more reading. It is learning a few case studies in a shape you can lift straight into an answer, then practising that lift on one question at a time.

Most schools are back this week after half term, and mocks land somewhere between November and January depending on your school. That makes now a good moment to set up a habit that is short, repeatable and calm. You do not need a marathon. You need one case, four facts and one practice question.

Why GCSE business revision needs case studies, not just definitions

Business Studies papers reward two things side by side. You need to know the terms, such as cash flow, market share or break even. You also need to apply them to a business in front of you and judge what it should do. Definitions alone earn the opening marks. The higher marks usually go to answers that use the context and reach a reasoned judgement.

A case study you know well gives you a model for that thinking. When you have already worked out why one business chose to expand, or why another struggled with cash flow, you can spot the same pattern in an unfamiliar business on the paper. You are not copying a memorised answer. You are using a worked example to think faster.

GCSE business case studies: where to get them

Use the cases you already have. Your teacher's slides, your class notes and the examples in your textbook were chosen to fit your course. Your own exam board's specification and its sample materials also show the kind of business contexts that appear. Check your own board's specification for which topics you need. Two rules keep your cases useful and honest:

  • Stick to what your class gave you. If a case came with figures, use those figures. Do not invent numbers to make a point sound stronger.
  • Keep it neutral. If you use a real company as an example, keep to plain, public facts. You are revising a business decision, not writing an advert.

Build a four-line fact bank for each case

For every case, write a short card with four lines:

  • Context. What kind of business is it, roughly how big, and what does it sell?
  • Problem. What challenge or opportunity did it face?
  • Decision. What did it choose to do?
  • Outcome. What happened next, and was it a good call?

That is it. Four lines, written in your own words. If a card runs longer than the front of a revision card, cut it back. The point is to carry the shape of the story, not every detail.

Then add one line underneath: the theory it shows. For example, a business that cut prices to win customers might illustrate price elasticity. A business that took on a loan to open a second site might illustrate sources of finance and risk. That link between the story and the term is what turns a fact into an answer.

Five to eight good cards across your topics is plenty for now. You can add more as your course moves on.

How to revise GCSE business with one question at a time

Once you have a few cards, practise using them. Keep each sitting short.

  1. Pick one question from a past paper or your teacher's practice sheet. A medium-length question is a good start.
  2. Read the context in the question carefully. Underline the business type, the problem and anything the question tells you about money, staff or customers.
  3. Choose one card from your fact bank that shows a similar pattern. You are not going to write about that business. You are using it to remind yourself how the theory works.
  4. Write your answer about the business in the question. Use its name or description. Use its numbers if it gives any.
  5. Finish with a judgement if the question asks for one. Say what the business should do and give the main reason.
  6. Mark it with your teacher's mark scheme or feedback. Note one thing you missed, such as using the context, or giving a reason after a point.

Then stop. One question done well, with one fix noted, beats four rushed answers you never check.

If a mistake keeps coming back, add it to your error log so you can see the pattern over a few weeks.

Use the context, every time

The most common slip in Business Studies answers is writing a perfect textbook paragraph that could be about any business. Markers want to see that you have read the case in front of you. A quick check helps. After each point, ask: does this sentence mention the business, its situation or its numbers? If not, add a short phrase that ties it back.

For example, instead of "Advertising can increase sales", try "Advertising in the local paper could bring more customers to the café during quiet weekday mornings." Same idea, but now it belongs to the question.

Stop before you script whole essays

It is tempting to write out long model answers and learn them word for word. Try not to. A scripted answer is built for one question, and the paper will almost always ask something slightly different. You end up forcing a memorised paragraph where it does not fit.

Keep your preparation at the level of cards and patterns. Know your four lines, know the theory each case shows, and practise building a fresh answer each time. That keeps your thinking flexible, which is exactly what the longer questions test.

Mix recall into short sessions

Reading a card is not the same as remembering it. A better test is to cover the card and say the four lines out loud, or write them from memory, then check. This kind of active recall takes a few minutes and shows you which cases are solid and which need another look.

A simple week might look like this:

  • Two short sessions: recall three cards each, from memory, then check.
  • One practice session: one question, one card, one fix noted.
  • One quick review: look back at your error log and pick one habit to work on next time.

That fits alongside your other subjects without taking over the evening.

A quick checklist for your first week back

  • Gather the case studies from your class notes and textbook.
  • Write a four-line card for five of them, plus the theory each one shows.
  • Practise one question using the context, and mark it.
  • Note one fix, then stop.
  • Ask your teacher if you are unsure which cases or topics matter most for your course.

GCSE business revision does not have to be heavy. A handful of well-shaped case studies, and the habit of applying them one question at a time, will give you something real to work with when the mocks arrive. Start with one card today.
