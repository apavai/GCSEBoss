What to Do in Summer After GCSEs: Job, Bridging Work, or Just Rest?

The exams are over, the timetable on the fridge is finally redundant, and a new question has quietly moved to the top of the pile: what now? Here's a calm way to think it through, without turning a free summer into one more thing to get right.

If you're trying to work out what to do in summer after GCSEs, you're in good company, and you've probably noticed the question carries more weight than it should. The written window closed only days ago. Your teenager has spent the best part of a year being told that this stretch matters enormously, and now, abruptly, there's nothing in the diary. Into that gap rushes a very parental instinct: surely they should be doing something. A job. Some prep for September. Anything but ten weeks of lying on the sofa watching the ceiling.

That instinct isn't wrong, exactly. But it helps to start by deflating the stakes. There is no single correct way to spend this summer, and the choice you make now won't quietly decide anything about results day or sixth form. What you're really doing is helping your teenager spend a long, unusually open stretch of time in a way that leaves them in good shape for the autumn. That's a much gentler brief than "make it count", and it's the one worth holding on to.

What to do in summer after GCSEs: three honest options

Strip away the noise and almost every plan falls into one of three buckets: a summer job, a bit of bridging work for the next stage, or genuine rest. The useful thing to know up front is that all three are legitimate. None is the responsible choice that makes the others a cop-out. A teenager who spends August stacking shelves, one who reads a couple of the books on their A-level list, and one who mostly sleeps and sees friends can all arrive in September perfectly well. The decision isn't between a good option and a bad one. It's about which fits your particular teenager, this particular summer.

Option one: a summer job

A summer job is the option that tends to win parental approval on sight, and for fair reasons. A few shifts a week gives shape to otherwise shapeless days, a little money of their own, and (quietly) a first taste of being answerable to someone who isn't a parent or a teacher. For a sixteen-year-old, learning that you turn up on time because other people are relying on you is worth more than it looks. The work itself rarely matters; a café, a campsite, a bit of cash-in-hand gardening for a neighbour all do the same job.

Two gentle caveats. Most formal employment has age and hours rules for under-eighteens, so it's worth a quick check on what's allowed locally before anyone gets their hopes up. And a job that swallows the entire summer can tip from "useful structure" into "no actual break", which rather defeats the point. A few shifts a week, with real days off around them, is usually the sweet spot.

Option two: a little bridging work

"Bridging work" sounds more formal than it needs to. For most teenagers it means nothing more than a light touch of preparation for what comes next: reading one of the set texts for English Literature, watching a few explainer videos on a topic they'll meet early in an A-level course, or brushing up the maths that a science or economics subject quietly assumes. Some sixth forms and colleges send out a bridging pack precisely for this; if yours has, it's a ready-made answer to the question.

The trap here is scale. Bridging work is a gentle on-ramp, not a head start to be won. An hour here and there across the summer is plenty, and it works best when it's about curiosity rather than obligation: a documentary, a podcast, a book they'd half like to read anyway. The moment it starts to feel like term has secretly restarted in July, it's doing more harm than good. If your teenager is the type who finds an empty calendar unsettling, a small, self-directed project like this can be genuinely steadying.

Option three: actual, unapologetic rest

This is the option that makes parents twitchy, so it's worth saying plainly: rest is a real plan, not the absence of one. Your teenager has just come through a sustained period of pressure, and the tiredness that follows is often physical as much as anything. A summer spent sleeping properly, seeing friends, getting outside, and slowly remembering what they enjoy when no one is assessing it is not wasted time. It's recovery, and it's frequently exactly what a depleted sixteen-year-old needs before stepping up a gear in September.

"Rest" doesn't have to mean a closed bedroom door and a permanently lit screen, of course. The version that leaves people feeling better tends to involve daylight, some movement, and other humans. But the underlying point stands: a teenager who does very little this summer and arrives at sixth form rested has not fallen behind. They've done the thing that lets them keep up.

How to decide it together

The good news is that these options aren't mutually exclusive: most real summers are a blend, perhaps a few weeks of shifts, a fortnight away from everything, and the odd evening with a book that's vaguely relevant to September. So rather than handing down a plan, the more useful move is a short, low-stakes conversation. What would they actually like the summer to look like? Is there anything they're quietly worried about for the next stage? Would a bit of money, or a bit of structure, or simply a proper rest do them the most good right now? You're not negotiating a contract. You're helping them notice their own preferences, which at sixteen is a skill in itself.

One steer worth offering, lightly: a summer with a little of each tends to age better than one that's all of any single thing. All rest and no structure can curdle into restlessness by August; all work and no break leaves them starting the new year already flat. A loose shape (not a timetable) is usually enough.

A quiet word on the weeks before results day

Results day sits at the far end of this summer (Thursday 20 August this year), and it can cast a longer shadow than it deserves. You don't need to organise the whole break around it, and you certainly don't need to fill July with revision-flavoured activity "just in case". Whatever your teenager does over the next few weeks (job, bridging work, or a well-earned stretch of doing very little), none of it changes a grade that's already been written. The kindest framing for everyone is the simplest one: the exams are done, the summer is theirs, and the only real task is to arrive at the autumn rested, steady, and glad to be there.
