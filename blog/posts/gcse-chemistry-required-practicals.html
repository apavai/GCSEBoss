GCSE Chemistry Required Practicals: How to Revise Them as You Go

Your GCSE chemistry required practicals happen in class across the course, often months before anyone asks you about them in an exam. The easiest time to revise one is the same week you do it, while you still remember what the kit looked like and what went wrong. This guide gives you a quick routine for doing exactly that.

Autumn term is full of practical work. The lesson ends, the next topic starts, and by mock season that practical can feel like it happened to someone else. A few minutes on GCSE chemistry required practicals straight afterwards stops that.

Why practicals are worth revising straight away

Exam questions on practicals aren't only "describe the method". They ask why a step matters, which variable you'd change, what would happen if something went wrong, or how you'd make the results more accurate. You can't answer those from a vague memory of a lesson you had in October.

The good news is that the lesson has already done half the work. A short session that evening or that weekend turns it into notes you'll actually use later in the year.

Know which GCSE chemistry required practicals are on your course

There isn't one list that fits everyone. The practicals you need depend on your exam board and on whether you're taking separate Chemistry or Combined Science, and the separate Chemistry and Combined Science lists are different. That means a friend's list, or a list you find online, might not match yours.

The name changes too. Some boards call them required practicals, others call them core practicals or practical activities, and some courses let schools choose which activities to run.

So start with the right list. Ask your teacher for the practicals on your course, or find them in your specification. Stick it at the front of your chemistry notes and tick each one off once you've made your revision page.

The ten-minute routine after each practical

Chemistry practicals GCSE revision works best in small chunks. After each practical, make one page or one revision card with the same five headings every time.

  • Aim. One sentence on what you were trying to find out or make.
  • Method in five lines. The key steps in order, written so someone who missed the lesson could follow them. Leave out the tiny details and keep the steps that change the result.
  • Variables. The independent variable (what you changed), the dependent variable (what you measured) and the control variables (what you kept the same).
  • Likely errors. One or two things that could make the results less accurate, and how you'd reduce them.
  • One exam-style question. Write a question you'd expect to see, then answer it from your notes a few days later.

Using the same headings each time means you can lay your pages side by side before mocks and spot patterns, because lots of practicals share the same kinds of errors and questions.

A worked example

Say you've just done a practical on how concentration affects the rate of a reaction, where a solution slowly turns cloudy and you time how long it takes for a cross drawn under the flask to disappear. Your page might look like this.

Aim: Find out how changing the concentration of one reactant affects the rate of reaction.

Method in five lines: Measure a set volume of one solution into a conical flask. Put the flask on a piece of paper with a cross drawn on it. Add a set volume of the second solution and start the timer. Stop the timer when you can no longer see the cross. Repeat with different concentrations.

Variables: Independent is the concentration. Dependent is the time taken for the cross to disappear. Control variables include the volumes, the temperature and using the same cross.

Likely errors: Deciding exactly when the cross has disappeared is a judgement, so results can vary. Using the same person to watch each time, repeating each concentration and working out a mean all help.

Exam-style question: "Explain why the same person should watch the cross each time." Different people may judge the moment the cross disappears differently, so using one observer makes the results more consistent.

Practicals you might meet

Your list is the one that counts, but these often appear on GCSE chemistry courses.

  • Making a salt. The steps from reactants to dry crystals, why you add the solid until no more reacts, how you remove the extra solid and how you get crystals to form.
  • Electrolysis. What forms at each electrode and how you'd identify any gas produced.
  • Temperature changes. How you'd measure the change and how you'd reduce heat loss to the surroundings.
  • Chromatography. Why the start line is drawn in pencil, why the solvent must start below the line, and how you'd use the results to compare substances.
  • Titration, if it's on your course. How to read the burette and why you swirl the flask and add the solution drop by drop near the end.
  • Ion tests, if they're on your course. Which test goes with which ion and what a positive result looks like. A small table works well here.

If your list is different, follow your course.

Turning your notes into GCSE chemistry practical questions

Once you've got a few pages, test yourself. GCSE chemistry practical questions usually come in a handful of types:

  • Describe the method, or put the steps in the right order.
  • Name the independent, dependent and control variables.
  • Explain why a particular step is done.
  • Suggest an improvement to make the results more accurate.
  • Work with results: spot a result that doesn't fit, calculate a mean or describe a pattern on a graph.

When a question says "explain", give the reason as well as the step. "Use a lid" is a step. "Use a lid to reduce heat loss to the surroundings" is an explanation. That is the same describe vs explain split as other papers.

Your teacher can give you practice questions that match your course. Use them after you've made your page, so you're checking what you remember.

Revise on paper, not at home in the kitchen

Revising a practical means going over the method, the variables and the errors on paper. It doesn't mean doing experiments at home. Lab chemicals and equipment belong in a school lab with a teacher there.

If you'd like to picture it again, draw the apparatus and label it. It's one of the best ways to revise a practical, and exam questions often ask for exactly that kind of diagram.

One more thing to check with your teacher: which chemistry equations and calculations you need to know. Learn those from your own notes rather than expecting them to be given to you in the exam.

The short version

Get the list of practicals on your course. After each one, spend ten minutes on a page with the same five headings: aim, method, variables, errors and one exam-style question.
