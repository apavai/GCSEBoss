Waiting for GCSE Results: How to Get Through the Last Few Days

Three days to go, nothing left to revise, and time doing that thing where it slows down on purpose. Here's how to spend them.

If you're waiting for GCSE results this week, you've probably noticed something odd: the waiting is harder than the exams were. In the exam period there was always something to do: a paper tomorrow, a topic to go over, a bag to pack. Now there's nothing. The work is finished, the papers are marked, and every grade you're going to get already exists on a spreadsheet somewhere. Results land on Thursday 20 August 2026. Between now and then, there is genuinely nothing you can do to change them, which is either the most annoying sentence you'll read today, or a slightly freeing one, depending on the hour.

This isn't a piece about dates and times. Plenty of those exist, including our own guide to results day timings. This is about the three ordinary days in front of you, and how to spend them without grinding yourself down.

Waiting for GCSE results is its own weird thing

The flat, restless feeling a lot of people get this week isn't a sign that something's wrong with you. It's what happens when a long stretch of pressure ends but the outcome hasn't arrived yet. Your brain has been in "there's a thing coming" mode since roughly April. It doesn't switch off just because the papers stopped. It just runs out of anything useful to point at, and starts pointing at Thursday instead.

That's why the days feel long and the evenings feel longer, why you can be completely fine at 2pm and slightly wired at midnight, and why "don't think about it" is such spectacularly useless advice. You can't not think about it. What you can do is give the next three days some shape, so your head has something else to be busy with.

Three days is short. Treat it like three normal days.

The single most useful reframe: this isn't a countdown, it's Monday, Tuesday and Wednesday. Three days you'd otherwise have spent in the ordinary way. So spend them in the ordinary way. See people. Go out. Sleep in, but not until 3pm. The further your body clock drifts, the rougher Thursday morning feels, and there's no upside to arriving at results day running on four hours and a lot of scrolling.

Make loose plans rather than no plans. An empty day with nothing in it will fill itself with the thing you're trying not to think about. A day with a walk, a shift, a film, a mate coming over, or a bus into town in it will mostly not.

What actually helps between now and Thursday

A few things that people consistently say made the wait easier:

Move about a bit. You don't need a fitness plan. A walk, a swim, a kickabout, cycling somewhere pointless: anything that gets you outside and slightly out of breath takes the edge off the restlessness better than almost anything else.

Be around people who aren't talking about results. Your friends are all in the same week, which is comforting right up until the group chat becomes a rolling forecast. If it's tipping into that, mute it for a few hours. Nobody in that chat has more information than you do.

Do something with an ending. Finish a series, build the thing, beat the level, cook something ambitious. Small completions are weirdly satisfying when you're stuck waiting on a big one.

Sort Thursday morning out now. Not the outcome: the logistics. Check what time your school opens, whether you're collecting in person or getting results another way, and how you're getting there. Schools set their own arrangements, so check with yours rather than assuming it's the same as your cousin's. Doing that on Monday means Wednesday night has one less loose end in it.

What to skip

Grade boundary hunting. Boundaries are set after all the papers are marked and published on results day morning. Anything you find before then is someone guessing. Reverse-engineering a mark you half-remember from a paper you sat in May is a genuinely bad use of a Tuesday.

Re-sitting the papers in your head. Everyone does this: the question you're suddenly sure you misread, the one where you wrote the right thing in the wrong box. You cannot audit an exam from memory. Your recall of a paper eleven weeks ago is not evidence.

Big decisions. Sixth form, college, apprenticeships, plan B: none of that needs deciding before Thursday, and most of it doesn't need deciding on Thursday either. If you want to know what routes exist so you feel less in the dark, have a calm read of the options after GCSEs now, while it's abstract. Then put it down.

If it doesn't go the way you're hoping

Worth saying plainly, once, and then leaving alone: whatever Thursday brings, there is a route from it. Papers can be looked at again through your school, and English and Maths can be sat again in November if that's what makes sense. We've written both of those up already (reviews and appeals and November resits) precisely so you don't have to research them at 8am on results day. Knowing the door exists is enough for now. You don't have to walk through it in advance.

If the waiting is genuinely getting to you

There's ordinary results-week restlessness, and then there's something heavier: not sleeping at all, not eating, dreading the day in a way that's sitting on your chest. If that's closer to where you are, tell someone this week rather than white- knuckling it to Thursday. A parent, an older sibling, a mate, your form tutor, the person at your school who does wellbeing. If it's easier to say it to someone who isn't in your life, Childline is free and open at any hour on 0800 1111. Saying it out loud almost always makes the week smaller. That's not a dramatic step; it's just a sensible one.

You've done the work

Here's the honest summary of waiting for GCSE results: it's boring, it's slightly uncomfortable, and it ends on Thursday. Nothing you do between now and then changes a single mark, which means you are (for the first time in months) completely off the hook. Sleep normally. Eat properly. See your friends. Get your Thursday morning logistics sorted so you're not sorting them at midnight on Wednesday.

And then go and have three normal days. You've done the work. There's nothing left to revise. You'll be fine.
