After GCSEs, What to Do When the Last Exam Is Finished: A Parent's Guide to the Flat Week

The last paper is almost here. After weeks of early starts, late revision and a kitchen calendar covered in exam dates, it's all about to just… stop. And the week that follows is rarely what anyone expects.

If you've started wondering after GCSEs what to do with the strange, empty week that's coming, you're already a step ahead of where most of us are. The honest answer is that the right move is usually much smaller than it feels like it should be. The exams have been the organising fact of your household for the best part of two months. When that suddenly lifts, the most common reaction in a teenager isn't fireworks and relief. It's a sort of flatness — a few aimless days that can quietly worry a parent who was expecting their child to bounce. So before the last paper is even sat, it's worth knowing what's normal, what helps, and what to gently leave alone.

After GCSEs, what to do first: usually, less than you think

Here's the reassuring headline: in the first few days, doing very little is exactly the right thing. Your teenager has just spent weeks in a state of low-level alertness — papers to sit, content to hold in their head, a clock always ticking somewhere. The body and brain don't switch out of that overnight. The instinct to immediately fill the gap with plans, projects or a "right, what now?" conversation is well-meant, but it tends to land on someone who has nothing left in the tank. Rest first. The bigger conversations can wait a week, and they'll go far better once everyone's slept.

Why the flat week happens

The anticlimax is a real, predictable thing, and it helps to understand the mechanics of it. For weeks there's been a clear purpose, a daily structure and a steady trickle of adrenaline. The moment the last exam ends, all three vanish at once. What's left is a strange vacuum: no timetable, no next deadline, and a slightly deflated feeling that the thing everyone's been building towards is simply… over. Add genuine tiredness on top, and you get a teenager who sleeps until noon, drifts around the house, and can't seem to settle to anything. That's not a warning sign. That's a nervous system finally allowed to stand down.

What the flat week actually looks like

It varies, but the shape is familiar. Long lie-ins and a body clock that's drifted hours later than yours. A lot of phone, a lot of gaming, a lot of lying on the sofa doing not very much. Short tempers or unusual quiet. A friend group that's suddenly always out, or suddenly never. Some teenagers replay specific questions and want to talk through how a paper went; others slam the door on the whole subject and don't want it mentioned. None of this is a problem to be solved. It's decompression, and most of it passes on its own within a week or two as a new, looser summer rhythm takes over.

What helps

A few low-key things make the flat week easier without forcing it. Keep one or two gentle anchors in the day — a shared meal, a walk, a small errand run together — so there's a little shape without a schedule. Say yes to the easy, restorative stuff: seeing friends, sleeping in, a film night, the kind of low-stakes fun that's been crowded out for months. Feed them well; appetite and mood are more linked than we like to admit. And keep your own expectations modest. A teenager who spends three days mostly horizontal after their final GCSE is not being lazy. They're recovering, and recovery is allowed to be unproductive.

It also helps to lower the temperature on conversation generally. You don't need to debrief every exam, audit how revision went, or start mapping out results day — all of that can wait, and most of it will feel very different in a fortnight anyway. If your teenager wants to talk about a paper, follow their lead and listen; if they don't, let the subject drop without making the silence mean something. Plenty of parents find the most useful thing they say in this first week is almost nothing about exams at all. A bit of ordinary, undemanding company — sitting in the same room, a takeaway, a shared show — does more to help someone decompress than any well-intentioned plan for the summer ever will.

What to avoid

Two traps are worth naming. The first is rushing to fill every day — lining up jobs, courses, reading lists and improving activities so the summer "isn't wasted." There's time for some of that later, but front-loading it onto an exhausted teenager in week one usually backfires. The second trap is reading the flatness itself as a problem: treating the low mood, the late nights and the aimlessness as something that needs fixing or worrying about out loud. Most of the time, naming it as a worry is what turns an ordinary slump into a tense one. The kindest thing you can do is let the flat week be flat, and trust that it lifts.

When flatness is worth a gentle second look

Almost always, this is just decompression and it resolves itself. Occasionally, though, a low patch settles in and stays — flatness that's still there well into the second or third week, a complete withdrawal from friends, sleep that's badly broken rather than just shifted late, or a mood that feels heavier than tiredness explains. If that's the picture, lead with warmth, not interrogation. Something as simple as "you've seemed a bit low since the exams finished — anything on your mind?" opens a door without putting them on the spot. And if your instinct keeps nagging, a quiet word with your GP is always a reasonable step. Trusting that instinct isn't overreacting; it's just parenting.

The hard part is genuinely done

It's easy, in the flat week, to feel as though something's gone slightly wrong — that the relief you both expected hasn't quite arrived. It will. The deflation is just the comedown from a long, demanding stretch, and it passes. So when you're turning over after GCSEs what to do next, the gentlest and most accurate answer is: not much, not yet. Let them sleep, feed them, keep one or two easy anchors in the day, and give the big questions about the summer and beyond a week or two to come into focus on their own. The exams are over. The work is done. For now, that really is enough — and so are you.
