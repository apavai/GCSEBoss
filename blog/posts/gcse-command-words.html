GCSE Command Words: What State, Describe, Explain and Evaluate Actually Want

GCSE command words are the short verbs at the start of a question. They tell you the shape of the answer, not how clever you need to sound.

Week three is when teachers start dropping exam-style questions into class after a fortnight of content. The trap with GCSE command words is writing the same paragraph for every verb. "Describe" and "explain" are not twins. Neither is "state" the same as "evaluate."

You do not need a fancy vocabulary list. You need five plain meanings and one worked contrast you can reuse across subjects.

What GCSE command words are asking for

The command word is usually the first word you should circle. Not the topic noun. The verb.

It answers one question: what job is this answer meant to do?

  • State wants a short fact or name. No story.
  • Describe wants what something looks like, does, or is made of. Features, not reasons.
  • Explain wants why or how. Link cause to effect.
  • Compare wants both sides in the same answer: likenesses and differences.
  • Evaluate wants a judgement after you have weighed points, not a list that stops in the middle.

Boards and subjects use these in slightly different ways. Treat the list as a habit, not a promise of marks. GCSE Boss is not affiliated with or endorsed by any exam board.

State: get in, answer, stop

State means give the thing asked for. A name, a value, a definition in one line.

If the question says "State the type of bond in sodium chloride," you write the type. You do not narrate the whole bonding chapter.

People lose time on state questions by adding an explain paragraph "just in case." Markers are not awarding you for padding a short command. Put the fact, move on.

Describe vs explain: the contrast that saves papers

This is the one mix-up that shows up in almost every subject.

Describe = what is there / what happens. Features, steps, appearance, process without digging into why.

Explain = why it happens / how it works. You need a link word in your head: because, so, therefore, which means.

Worked contrast, science-shaped:

Describe photosynthesis: green plants take in carbon dioxide and water and make glucose and oxygen using light.

Explain why a leaf is broad and thin: a large surface area and a short distance for gases help the leaf take in carbon dioxide and release oxygen quickly.

Same topic. Different jobs. If you write the "explain" answer under "describe," you may still say true things and miss the shape the question asked for. If you write "describe" under "explain," you list features and never make the causal link.

English and humanities use the same split. Describe the setting. Explain how the writer creates tension. Compare is a third job again.

Compare: both sides in one answer

Compare wants A and B in the same breath. Similarities and differences, not two separate mini essays that never meet.

A useful shape:

  • One clear similarity
  • One clear difference
  • A second difference or a tighter similarity if the marks look bigger

Do not write everything you know about A, then everything about B, and hope the marker joins them. Do the joining yourself with phrases like "both…", "unlike…", "whereas…".

Evaluate: judgement after weighing

Evaluate is not "write a long description then stop." It is "weigh points, then say which side is stronger or what matters most."

A workable habit:

  • Point for
  • Point against (or a limit)
  • Clear overall judgement tied to the question

You do not need fancy critique language. You need a decision sentence. "Overall…" or "On balance…" is enough if the points before it are real.

In science this might mean weighing two methods. In English it might mean how far a reading of a character is supported. Same spine: weigh, then judge.

How to practise GCSE command words this week

Do not wait for a full mock paper. Steal five minutes after a lesson.

  1. Open the homework or worksheet.
  2. Circle every command word.
  3. Say out loud what shape it wants (fact, features, reason, both sides, judgement).
  4. Write one sentence in that shape before you write the full answer.

If you keep writing the same "explain" paragraph for every verb, rewrite one of them as describe or state and feel how short it should be.

When a teacher returns a marked question, look at the verb first. Ask: did I answer the job, or did I dump topic knowledge? That is the same pause as an error log: one line on the miss, then move on.

What not to do

Do not turn this into a revision timetable. Do not promise yourself a grade from learning the verbs. Do not shame yourself for mixing describe and explain once. Fix the habit.

Do not invent a brand-new meaning for a word your teacher has already defined for that subject. Classroom glosses win for that paper.

Do not import panic from May. This is a week-three skills post: read the verb, match the shape, write less fluff. It is narrower than class test revision. The verb is the job, not the whole topic list.

Across subjects without rewriting the dictionary

Science leans on state, describe, explain, and sometimes evaluate of a method. English leans on describe, explain, compare, and evaluate of a reading. Geography and history lean hard on describe, explain, and compare of places, causes, or interpretations.

You do not need five different posters. You need the same pause: what job does this verb want today?

If a mark scheme in your subject uses a slightly different gloss, follow your teacher and that paper. The habit still holds. Circle the verb. Match the shape. Stop when the job is done.

When the question stacks two verbs

Some questions say "describe and explain" or "compare and evaluate." Do both jobs, in order, and leave a clear join between them.

Write the describe block first if that is the first verb. Then start a new paragraph for explain. Do not mash them into one muddy paragraph where the marker cannot see either shape.

If the marks look uneven, spend more lines on the heavier verb. That is judgment, not padding.

Parents who ask what you are doing

You can say: "I'm matching the verb to the answer shape." That is accurate. They do not need a lecture on assessment objectives.

If they want to help, they can read a question out and ask only "what does the first word want?" before you write.

The check before you hand work in

Ask three things:

  • Did I circle the command word?
  • Does my first sentence match that job?
  • Did I stop when the job was done?

That is how GCSE command words pay off. Not as a poster on the wall. As a two-second pause before you write.
