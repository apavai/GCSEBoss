Your Child Starts Year 11 Next Week: Three Conversations Worth Having First

There is a version of this weekend where nobody mentions school at all, and a version where somebody mentions it at the worst possible moment on Sunday night. There is a third version that takes about twenty minutes in total and makes the next four months noticeably calmer.

Most of the advice on how to support your child in Year 11 arrives in September, in the form of a letter from school with a lot of dates on it. By then the year has its own momentum and any conversation you start sounds like a reaction to something. The last weekend of the holiday is a better moment, because nothing has gone wrong yet and there is nothing to defend.

Three conversations. None of them is about how much revision they are going to do. That question goes badly in every house, because it asks a fifteen year old to commit in August to an effort level they cannot yet picture. These three are about the shape of the year instead.

How to support your child in Year 11 without turning into the revision police

The useful framing, and the one the parents and teachers we talk to keep landing on, is that your job this year is infrastructure rather than enforcement. Dates in a shared calendar. A room that works after nine at night. A sense of what is coming so nothing arrives as a surprise. The actual work is theirs, and any system that depends on you chasing it nightly will fail by half term, usually loudly.

Infrastructure conversations are also easier to have, because they are logistical. Nobody has to admit anything.

Conversation one: the mocks calendar, and what mocks are actually for

Mocks usually sit in November or December of Year 11, which is roughly ten to fifteen weeks after term starts. Some schools run a second set in February or March. Your school will confirm the exact window in the first few weeks, and it is worth asking rather than waiting, because everything else in the autumn hangs off those dates.

The conversation is not "mocks are coming, start revising". It is two sentences of context that most students genuinely do not have. First, mocks exist to tell everyone which topics are weak while there is still time to do something about it. Second, in a lot of schools they also feed predicted grades for sixth form and college applications, which is the part that actually matters to a teenager and the part nobody explains.

Say both. Then put the window in a shared calendar and leave it there. You have now given them a horizon without asking them for anything, which is a good position to be in.

Conversation two: what visibility looks like without the nightly interrogation

Every parent wants to know how it is going. Almost no teenager wants to be asked, and asking daily reliably produces the word "fine" and a closed door. The way out is to agree a form of visibility in advance, while it is still hypothetical and nobody is defensive.

What works, roughly in order of how well it holds up:

  • A fixed weekly check in. Ten minutes, same slot, usually Sunday. Three questions: what went well, what was rubbish, what is coming this week. Then it is done and you do not raise it again until the next one.
  • Something that shows you the picture without you asking. A shared calendar, the school's parent portal, or an app with a parent view. The point is that the information reaches you passively, so the relationship does not have to carry it.
  • An agreed escalation. One sentence: if something goes properly wrong, they tell you early and the response is help rather than a row. Worth saying out loud now, because it is worth nothing said for the first time in January.

Whatever you use, agree it together and tell them exactly what you can see. Visibility they know about is support. Visibility they discover is surveillance, and it costs you more than it gains you.

Conversation three: one agreement about phones that you can both live with

One agreement. Not a policy, not a list. The households where this goes well have a single rule that is specific, mutual, and small enough to keep.

Specific means "the phone charges in the kitchen overnight from Sunday to Thursday", not "less phone". Mutual means it applies to the room or the hour rather than only to them, so it reads as a household thing instead of a punishment. Small enough means you will still be enforcing it in week nine, which rules out most of the ambitious versions.

Let them pick between two options you can both accept. Agreement they had a hand in survives the autumn. An imposed rule gets litigated every evening until you drop it, and dropping it costs you more authority than never setting it.

Three dates worth putting in your own calendar

Not theirs. Yours, so you are not the last to know.

  • The mocks window, once school confirms it. Usually November or December.
  • Year 11 parents evening, which is often the first honest read you get on how the year is going.
  • 4 October 2026, if anyone in the house is sitting an English Language or Maths GCSE again in November. That is the entry deadline as it stands, and deadlines vary by a few days between boards and centres, so confirm with the exam centre rather than taking a date off the internet.

If the conversation goes badly

Sometimes it does. A shrug, a "we did this last year", a door. That is not a failed conversation, it is a conversation that has finished for now. Leave the calendar entry where it is and try one of the other two in a couple of weeks, ideally sideways in the car rather than head on at the table. The student version of this piece covers the same ground from their side, and is sometimes easier to send than to say.

What is worth avoiding is the version where the summer becomes the subject. Whatever did or did not happen in August is finished, and reopening it now buys you a worse September.

One thing before Monday

Ask which subjects they are least sure about. Not weakest, least sure. It is a lower stakes question and it usually gets a real answer, and it tells you where the year is likely to get difficult long before a mock result does.

Then leave it. Calendar set, visibility agreed, phone question settled while it was still theoretical. That is a good position to start Year 11 from, and it took one Sunday.
