GCSE Ratio Questions: Choosing the Right Quantities Before You Calculate

GCSE ratio questions often go wrong before any calculating starts. The arithmetic is usually fine. What trips people up is picking the wrong quantity to work with: dividing by the wrong total, mixing up a part with the whole, or comparing centimetres with metres. This guide is about the thirty seconds before you calculate, when you decide what each number in the question actually stands for.

Depending on your school, mocks might be a few weeks away or not until the new year, so use this alongside the topics your teacher has already covered. For GCSE ratio questions, the useful work happens before you reach for the calculator.

GCSE ratio questions start with what each number means

Before you do anything, read the question once just to label things. For every number, ask: what is this a number of? Sweets, pounds, people, grams, minutes? Then ask whether it describes one part of the ratio or the whole amount.

Take a bag of red and blue counters in the ratio 3:5. The 3 and the 5 are not counts of counters. They are parts. For every 3 red counters there are 5 blue ones. If the question later says there are 40 counters in the bag, that 40 is a whole. If it says there are 15 red counters, that 15 belongs to one part only.

Writing a tiny label above each number, like "R part", "B part" or "whole", takes a few seconds and stops you dividing the wrong thing later.

Part to part versus part to whole

This is the distinction that matters most.

  • Part to part compares one group with another group. Red to blue is 3:5.
  • Part to whole compares one group with everything. Red counters make up 3 out of every 8, so the fraction that is red is 3/8.

The 8 never appears in the ratio itself. You have to make it by adding the parts. A common slip is to see 3:5 and write the fraction as 3/5. That compares red with blue, not red with the whole bag.

A quick check: if a question asks "what fraction" or "what proportion", it almost always wants part to whole, so you will need the total number of parts. If it asks you to compare two groups directly, it is part to part.

Get the units the same first

A ratio only makes sense when both sides are measured the same way. If a question compares 50 cm with 2 m, convert first. 2 m is 200 cm, so the ratio is 50:200, which simplifies to 1:4. Leaving it as 50:2 gives a completely different answer.

The same goes for time (minutes and hours), money (pence and pounds) and mass (grams and kilograms). Circle any units in the question and make them match before you simplify.

Three common types of question

Not every ratio question works the same way, and it helps to recognise a few patterns rather than forcing one method onto everything. Here are three that come up often. Your own course and your teacher's examples should guide which you practise most.

You know the whole

Share £40 in the ratio 3:5.

  • Add the parts: 3 + 5 = 8 parts.
  • Find one part: £40 ÷ 8 = £5.
  • Multiply back: 3 × £5 = £15 and 5 × £5 = £25.
  • Check: £15 + £25 = £40.

Here the 40 is a whole, so you divide by the total number of parts.

You know one part

The ratio of boys to girls in a club is 2:3. There are 12 boys. How many girls are there?

  • The 12 matches the boys' part, which is 2 parts.
  • One part is 12 ÷ 2 = 6.
  • Girls are 3 parts, so 3 × 6 = 18.

The trap is dividing 12 by 5, as if 12 were the whole club. Labelling 12 as "boys part" before calculating makes that mistake much less likely.

You know the difference

Ali and Ben share some money in the ratio 4:7. Ben gets £15 more than Ali. How much does each get?

  • The difference in parts is 7 - 4 = 3 parts.
  • Those 3 parts are worth £15, so one part is £5.
  • Ali gets 4 × £5 = £20 and Ben gets 7 × £5 = £35.
  • Check: £35 - £20 = £15.

Here the £15 is neither a part nor the whole. It is the gap between two parts, so you work with the difference in the ratio.

Bar models make the parts visible

If you find ratio slippery, try drawing it. A bar model is just a row of equal boxes. For 3:5, draw 3 boxes for one group and 5 for the other. Now you can see that there are 8 boxes in total, and you can write the known amount against the right boxes.

If £40 covers all 8 boxes, each box is £5. If 12 covers 2 boxes, each box is 6.

Check your answer makes sense

Ratio answers are easy to sense-check, so build it in every time.

  • Do your shares add up to the whole you were given?
  • Is the bigger share attached to the bigger number in the ratio?
  • If you found a fraction, is it less than 1 when it should be?
  • Have you answered the actual question, or stopped at "one part"?

How to practise ratio this week

Short, focused practice works better than one long session.

  • Pick five ratio questions from your class work or your school's past papers.
  • Before calculating anything, label every number as a part, a whole or a difference.
  • Solve them, then mark them with the mark scheme your teacher has given you.
  • For any you got wrong, write one line on what went wrong: the wrong total, the wrong units, or stopping too early.

That one line is the useful bit. Over a few weeks, you will notice whether your slips are mostly about labelling, units or checking, and you can practise exactly that. A simple GCSE error log is a tidy place to keep these notes, and our guide on using a mark scheme explains how to read the marking without just checking the final number. If you want to mix ratio into wider practice without a calculator, the non-calculator maths guide has more ideas.

If ratio still feels unclear

Take one confusing question to your maths teacher and show them where you got stuck. Saying "I didn't know whether this number was a part or the whole" is a precise, useful question, and it is much easier for a teacher to help with than "I don't get ratio".

Ratio is a skill you build, not something you either have or don't. Label first, match the units, decide whether you need part to part or part to whole, then calculate and check. Do that on a handful of questions each week and the method starts to feel like yours.
